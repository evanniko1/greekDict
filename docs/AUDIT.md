# Lexorama (Λεξόραμα) — Adversarial Methodology Audit

**Date:** 2026-07-21 · **Commit audited:** `0565f04` (working tree clean) · **Artifact audited:** `data/db/lexorama.sqlite` (1,556,893,696 bytes) · **Mode:** read-only

All quantities below were measured directly against the shipped database or the tracked source tree. Every claim carries a `file:line` or a live query. Where a finding survived a second adversarial verification pass, the vote is recorded as `2/2`; `1/2` means one verifier confirmed and one could not fully reproduce the framing (the underlying measurement still held in every such case); `critic-sourced` means the finding came from the completeness pass and was measured but not double-verified.

## 1. Executive summary

**The single most important structural fact: the shipped 1.5 GB database was not produced by the code in this repository, and nothing in the repo can detect that.** `change_point_score` is NULL in 20,265/20,265 drift rows although the current `ingest_diachronic.py` writes it unconditionally. `drift_score_boot` is NULL in 20,265/20,265 rows although `bootstrap_drift.py:326` writes it. 10,251/16,803 classifier rows (61.0%) sit below the `CONF_FLOOR = 0.50` that `classify_domains.py:69` now enforces. 1,266 of the 14,232 bootstrap CIs recorded in HANDOFF survive (8.9%). There is no build manifest, no checksum, no dump-date pin, no `schema_version` table. Every number the site displays therefore describes a superseded method, while the methodology page next to it describes the current one. For a project whose entire differentiator is methodological honesty, this is the most damaging class of defect available, and it is not a subtle one.

**Second: the headline diachronic measurement is ~96% estimator noise.** Mean `distance_from_ref` for the news corpus: 2011 = 0.0000 (reference), 2013 = 0.1171, 2016 = 0.2809, then 2017 = 0.2813 … 2024 = 0.2914. The entire 2016→2024 evolution is +0.0105 — 3.6% of the final value. Parliament reaches 89% of its 2020 level by 1998. Two of the "slices" the news axis compares (2011 and 2013 under ±2-year pooling) resolve to the *identical* member set `[2011, 2013]`, so their measured distance of 0.1171 is a direct reading of the same-data noise floor. At the observed plateau slope (0.0013/yr) that floor is worth ~89 years of "semantic change". Drift is additionally anti-correlated with corpus frequency at Spearman −0.547 (news) / −0.557 (parliament), and the `min_pm=1.0` gate advertised as the Dubossarsky control removes 158 of 11,835 news lemmas (1.3%) and leaves ρ = −0.544. The Gonen neighbour-overlap measure labelled "frequency-robust" in the UI is confounded at ρ = −0.52 and correlates ρ = 0.79 with cosine drift — it is not an independent check.

**Third: the corpus-attribution primitive is broken in three independent ways, and it feeds four published analyses.** Nothing validates that a Wiktionary "form" is a word. The Greek guillemets `«` and `»` are indexed as inflected forms of **περιπλέκω**, giving it 2,928,583 news tokens (3,500.8 per million) and rank 4 on the user-facing news-keyness list with G² = 4,757,274.6 and dp_news = 0.04 — the dispersion gate certifies punctuation as exemplary. The adjective **αισώπειος** owns the bare endings `ο`, `ος`, `ου`, `ων`, absorbing 8,542,487 parliament tokens (0.81% of the corpus) and becoming the **#1 falling word of Greek** under the current trends code (Theil–Sen −405.97). And `ingest_frequency.py:97-98` credits a surface's count to every matching index *row* rather than every distinct lemma, so per-lemma counts sum to **1,026% of the corpus** (2,584,088,301 against 251,785,885) — 33.6% of that is pure duplicate-row double-add. `MAX(zipf) = 8.615`; 225 lemmas exceed Zipf 7. Every Zipf value, band, frequency meter, keyness score, `min_pm` drift gate and trend slope is computed on these counts.

**Fourth: 33.9% of Greek verbs display active and mediopassive forms in the same cell.** Only 6,470 of 635,350 el-wiktionary verb forms (1.0%) carry a voice tag, so `inflection.ts:334` routes every mediopassive form into the Ενεργητική φωνή grid and `:216` joins it to the active form with `" / "`. σκοτώνω stores `έχω σκοτώσει` and `έχω σκοτωθεί` under byte-identical feature lists. 115,326 collapsed cells over 5,440 of 16,053 verb lemmas. For a Greek speaker this reads as the dictionary being simply wrong about the primary axis of the verb.

**Fifth: the accent-folding primary key has permanently deleted headwords.** `schema.sql:15` declares `UNIQUE (normalized_lemma, pos)` over the accent-*folding* `normalize()`. Direct probe: **ποτέ, νομός, δουλεία, μονός, κάλος, χαλί, στοιχειό, ωρά all have 0 lemma rows and 0 search_index surfaces.** The stated invariant "accent-homographs are disambiguated at RANKING, not in normalize" is unimplementable as built — `main.py:309`'s `(si.surface = ?) DESC` tiebreak can only order rows that exist. 7,190 normalized keys still carry >1 display spelling, but only because the two words happened to differ in POS.

**Sixth: 75.4% of the "dictionary" is proper names.** 336,873 of 446,840 lemmas are `pos='name'`, 310,357 of those glossed «επώνυμο». The discovery endpoints filter them (`main.py:1027, 1129, 1162, 1334, 1442, 1505`); search, word pages, frequency, collocations and examples do not. The surname **«Να»** carries Zipf 7.58, rank 45, 9,582,861 tokens and the particle's collocations (πρέπει, μπορεί, προκειμένου, αναμένεται). Every DB-wide coverage percentage quoted in HANDOFF against 446,840 is understated ~4x relative to actual vocabulary (≈100,518 content lemmas).

**Seventh: there is no evaluation of the flagship feature, and no CI at all.** A repo-wide grep for `precision@|P@1|MRR|nDCG|recall@|gold set|eval set` across all `.py`/`.md`/`.tsx` returns **zero hits**. No `.github/`, no `Makefile`, no `pytest.ini`, no `pyproject.toml` — no CI of any kind across 86 tracked files. The 87 tests import exactly four symbols from the API and zero of its 13 route handlers. 11 pipeline modules totalling 2,016 LOC — including `paradigm_engine.py`, which owns the advertised ≥0.97 gate — have no test. `tests/test_normalization.py:11` asserts the accent folding that deletes headwords as *desired* behaviour, so no test could ever catch finding five.

### What is genuinely strong

This is not a bad project, and several parts of it are better than the published literature's median.

- **Provenance where the schema enforces it.** All 16 sourced tables declare `source TEXT NOT NULL` and the live DB has **0 NULL/blank source values across 541,759 senses, 3,216,685 forms, 291,208 relations and 294,045 collocations**. Generated paradigm cells are visually flagged to the user (`InflectionTables.tsx:74-80`, tooltip «Αλγοριθμικά παραγόμενος τύπος — όχι από πηγή»). Most aggregators do not do this at all.
- **The licensing wall holds.** `grep -rniE "triantafyllid|τριανταφυλλ|academy of athens|χρηστικ|e-lexicon|greek-language.gr"` across `pipelines/ services/ apps/` returns **zero hits**, and every `source` value in the DB is an accounted-for open source. The Wiktionary axis specifically is done well: dual CC BY-SA 4.0 / GFDL correctly identified (`ingest_kaikki.py:50,60`), explicit modification notices in Greek and English satisfying §3(a)(1)(B), and the correct (cheaper) CC BY-SA outbound path chosen over GFDL.
- **Method *design* tracks the literature.** Per-slice word2vec + orthogonal Procrustes is the SemEval-2020-validated family; using the full trajectory rather than endpoints is exactly Shoemark et al. 2019's finding; implementing Gonen 2020 alongside cosine is more than most papers do; separating effect size (Hardie log-ratio) from significance (Dunning G²) in keyness is Gabrielatos's prescription; replacing an OLS t-test with Theil–Sen + Mann–Kendall + BH-FDR was the right move; and the "one corpus per axis, never pool registers" rule (`ingest_diachronic.py:14-17`) correctly refuses to let Procrustes align register differences.
- **Honest disclosure instincts.** The amber warning box on `MethodologyPage.tsx:215-222` telling users the domain classifier is ~0.56 accurate is real honesty, rarer than it should be. Choosing per-class *precision* as the classifier headline is the right metric. The gold/classifier provenance split (`score 1.0, source 'wiktionary-tag'` vs `source 'classifier'`) is enforced end-to-end.
- **SQL hygiene.** Every statement in `main.py` is properly parameterized; the only f-string interpolation is generated `?` placeholders. The hot `/api/search` path is correctly indexed (`SEARCH si USING INDEX idx_search_norm`, <1 ms) and WAL is on.
- **The core Greek retrieval path actually works.** Running the evaluation the project lacks (800 known-item queries, replaying `main.py:296-311` verbatim): **P@1 = 0.915, MRR@10 = 0.956, R@10 = 1.000, p50 latency 0.05 ms**. That number had never existed before this audit.

### The one-sentence verdict

Lexorama is a well-architected, correctly-licensed, provenance-conscious lexical aggregator whose analytics layer makes quantitative claims — semantic drift, change-point years, statistical significance, distinctive vocabulary, Zipf frequency — that the underlying data cannot support, presented through a methodology page describing code that never touched the shipped database. Fix the corpus-attribution primitive and the data/code drift, cut the analytics that cannot be defended, and the dictionary underneath is genuinely good.

## 3. Detailed findings

### 3.1 Statistical inference validity

#### F7 (high) — Reported drift is ~96% estimator noise floor; the significance control cannot see it · *1/2*

**Claim.** The cosine drift score is dominated by an additive, immediately-saturating between-model/alignment artifact that is never subtracted, and the "no-change control" used to declare 87% of tested lemmas significant reproduces only a small sub-component of that artifact.

**Evidence.** Live DB, `diachronic_trajectory` mean `distance_from_ref` by year — news: 2011 = 0.0, 2013 = 0.1171, 2016 = 0.2809, then 2017 = 0.2813, 2018 = 0.2785, … 2024 = 0.2914. Mean `drift_score` (2011↔2024) = 0.2914, so only (0.2914 − 0.2809)/0.2914 = **3.6%** of the headline 13-year drift accrues after the second comparison slice. Parliament: 1989 = 0.0, 1990 = 0.1492 (47% of the final 0.3157 after **one year**), 1998 = 0.2801 (89%), 2020 = 0.3157 — 22 further years add 0.036. The code states the same thing: `ingest_diachronic.py:221` "The reference→first step is skipped (it's all model variance)" and `tests/test_change_point.py:35-36` "the 0→0.6 reference→first jump". The control null is `first_i ↔ first_j` (`bootstrap_drift.py:279-287`) — two models trained on resamples of the *same* slice with identical hyperparameters, identical vocabulary and identical size, so it captures sentence-resampling variance only. It holds constant corpus size, topic mix, vocabulary and alignment difficulty — exactly what differs between the two anchor slices (news token volume jumps 32.4M in 2018 to 108.9M in 2019 per `frequency_timeseries`). Result: `drift_significant=1` for **1,070/1,231** tested rows (news 82.6%, parliament 93.7%). Dubossarsky et al. 2017's control is a time-label shuffle across the whole corpus, not a within-slice resample, so the citation at `bootstrap_drift.py:14-17` and `MethodologyPage.tsx:93-97` does not describe what the code does.

**Why it matters.** Every user-facing drift number, every "biggest movers" list and the σημαντική/μη σημαντική chip are built on a quantity that is ~96% invariant to the time interval being measured. A word declared to have "changed meaning" has, on this evidence, mostly demonstrated that two word2vec models trained on different text are different.

**Fix.** Replace the within-slice bootstrap control with a permutation control that preserves corpus heterogeneity: randomly reassign documents to the two anchor bins (or shuffle year labels corpus-wide), retrain, build the null from that. Report drift as excess over the permutation null (or over the saturated plateau). Publish the plateau explicitly on MethodologyPage as a measured noise floor.

#### F8 (high) — `drift_significant`/`drift_q` are not a test: z divides by a replicate SD, not a standard error · *2/2*

**Claim.** `bootstrap_drift.py` computes `z = (mean(real) − mean(control)) / SD(control)` and feeds `norm.sf(z)` to Benjamini–Hochberg as if it were a p-value; under the null this statistic has SD √(2/K), not 1.

**Evidence.** `bootstrap_drift.py:309-312` — `sd_c = float(np.std(cs, ddof=1))`, `z = (boot_mean - mu_c) / sd_c`, `p_by_id[lid] = float(_norm.sf(z))`; passed to `_bh_fdr(p_by_id, alpha=0.05)` at `:315`. Monte Carlo (200,000 trials, real and control drawn from the same distribution = H₀): K=12 → z has sd **0.452** and P(p<0.05) = **9.4e-4**; K=100 → z sd 0.143, P(p<0.05) = 0.000000. The correct denominator for a difference of means is `sd·√(2/K)`, i.e. the code's denominator is √(K/2) too large — 2.45x at K=12, 7.07x at K=100. The rule reduces to "mean real drift exceeds mean control drift by 1.645 control SDs", a threshold that never tightens with K; p-values concentrate at 0.5 (5th/50th/95th percentiles 0.231/0.500/0.768 at K=12) rather than U(0,1), so BH cannot control FDR. Additional evidence the flag is decorative: no discovery endpoint filters on it (`main.py:1015-1033, 1115-1168, 1247-1252, 1438-1447` contain no `drift_significant`/`drift_q` predicate), and only 1,231 of 20,265 drift rows (6.1%) carry any flag, while `HANDOFF.md:564-566` publishes "9,003 CIs, 5,494 significant" (parliament) and "9,225 CIs, 3,752 significant" (news) versus the shipped 490/449 and 776/621 — an order of magnitude apart. `MethodologyPage.tsx:95-97` still describes the retired CI-non-overlap rule (`real_pct2.5 > ctrl_pct97.5`) that the code no longer implements.

**Why it matters.** The single strongest scientific claim the site makes rests on a statistic with no null distribution, a q-value with no FDR interpretation, published counts 10x larger than the data behind them, and a public methodology page describing a different test than the one that ran.

**Fix.** Either (a) build the null by permutation, compute p as the permutation tail probability, then apply BH; or (b) drop the p/q language and present the criterion honestly as a standardized effect size ("drift exceeds this word's own estimation noise by X SDs"). Regenerate the DB so the flag population matches the published counts, and rewrite `MethodologyPage.tsx:87-97`.

#### F9 (high) — Every shipped lemma gets a change-point year with the confidence gate never applied · *2/2*

**Claim.** `diachronic_drift` has `change_point_year` non-null for 20,255 of 20,265 rows and `change_point_score` NULL for all 20,265 — the #44 significance gate was never run — and the modal change point is an artifact of the saturation transient and of unequal year spacing.

**Evidence.** `SELECT sum(change_point_year IS NOT NULL), sum(change_point_score IS NOT NULL), COUNT(*) FROM diachronic_drift` → **20,255 / 0 / 20,265** (news 11,825/11,835; parliament 8,430/8,430). Distribution: news 2016 = 9,764 rows = **82.6%** of all news words; parliament ≤1996 = 40.3%, ≤1999 = 49.7%. Both coincide with the saturation region (news mean step into 2016 = 0.2809 − 0.1171 = **+0.164** versus +0.0004, −0.0028, +0.0042 for every later step — 40 to 400x larger). Structural cause: `change_point_year` (`ingest_diachronic.py:216-243`) takes raw differences `d1 - d0` at `:224` with **no division by the year gap**, while the news dense years are 2011, 2013, 2016, 2017, … — the 2013→2016 step spans 3 years and is the first scorable step. Re-running the *current gated* code on 3,000 real news trajectories still assigns 2016 to 50.9% of words (43.4% suppressed). Simulating the gate on stationary trajectories at the code's own assumed step noise of 0.05 (the hard floor at `:240`) gives a false change-point rate of 39.2% (11 slices) / 58.3% (31 slices). There is no null distribution, no uncertainty and no p-value for the change point anywhere in the tree; `MethodologyPage.tsx:249` concedes only that its significance is "not yet" estimated.

**Why it matters.** The site tells users a specific historical year is when a word's meaning shifted. For 82.6% of news words that year is 2016, chosen because the corpus has a three-year hole before it and the estimator is still saturating — and the guard written to suppress such calls has never touched the shipped data.

**Fix.** Normalize steps by Δyear; drop the reference-saturation region (or use difference-from-previous-slice instead of distance-from-fixed-reference); re-run ingest so `change_point_score` is populated and the z≥2 gate actually applies; calibrate `min_z` against a permutation null rather than a MAD heuristic with a hard-coded 0.05 floor. Until then, suppress the change-point display.

#### F10 (high) — `explore_trends` selects on R² then tests the same data, and ignores strong serial correlation · *2/2*

**Claim.** The trends endpoint filters candidates on R² ≥ 0.25 in SQL then computes the Mann–Kendall p-value and BH-FDR on the surviving series only — selective inference — while the MK variance carries no autocorrelation correction despite the test suite labelling it Hamed–Rao.

**Evidence.** `main.py:1339` `HAVING ... (sxy*sxy)/(sxx*syy) >= ?` (min_r2 default 0.25 at `:1297`), then `:1379` `p_by_id[lid] = _mann_kendall_p(pms)[0]` and `:1380` `_bh_fdr(p_by_id, alpha=0.05)` over exactly that selected set. Simulation under a **global null** (pure white noise, no trend anywhere): n=31 (parliament) — 0.4% of series pass R²≥0.25, and among survivors raw P(p<0.05) = **1.000** and BH at q≤0.05 rejects **100.0%**; n=11 (news) — 11.9% pass, raw P(p<0.05) = 0.336, BH rejects 0.0%. For parliament the realized FDR is 100%, not 5%. Separately, `_mann_kendall_p` (`main.py:816-843`) computes only the tie-corrected variance with no Hamed–Rao serial-correlation term, while `tests/test_trend_significance.py:53` heads the section "Mann–Kendall (Hamed–Rao)". Measured on the real input: OLS-residual lag-1 autocorrelation over 1,500 parliament lemmas (≥20 years, avg_pm≥1) has mean **+0.204**, median +0.177, 74.1% positive, 36.9% above +0.3. Simulated MK Type I error at α=0.05 under AR(1): n=31 → 0.053 (φ=0), 0.103 (φ=0.2), 0.175 (φ=0.4), 0.290 (φ=0.6). At the measured φ≈0.2 the parliament trend test runs at roughly twice its nominal size before selection effects.

**Why it matters.** The "rising / falling words" lists carry a `significant: true` badge and a q-value that `MethodologyPage.tsx:157-167` presents as FDR control. Under the measured data conditions a pure-noise vocabulary would be reported as 100% significant among everything the R² gate lets through.

**Fix.** Compute the MK p-value on ALL candidates before the R² gate and apply BH across that full set, using R² only for display/ranking. Implement the Hamed–Rao (or Yue–Wang) variance correction, or switch to a block-bootstrap p-value, and rename the docstring/tests to match. Report the measured residual autocorrelation alongside the trend.

#### F11 (high) — K=12 percentile CI has 82% coverage, and the displayed point estimate is a different estimator · *2/2*

**Evidence.** `_percentile_ci` (`bootstrap_drift.py:76-87`) calls `np.percentile(arr, 2.5/97.5)` with default linear interpolation. At K=12 the 2.5% index is 0.025·11 = 0.275, so the lower endpoint interpolates order statistics 1 and 2 of 12 (E[F] ≈ 0.098 — the interval spans ~80% of the bootstrap mass by construction). Estimator mismatch: `SELECT COUNT(*) FROM diachronic_drift WHERE drift_score_boot IS NOT NULL` = **0 of 20,265**, so the fallback at `main.py:953-954` always serves `drift_score` next to a CI built from resampled models; `SELECT COUNT(*) ... #### F43 (medium) — Keyness: significance gate passes 88.4% of candidates; the displayed top-30 is a winner's curse · *1/2*

**Claim.** The Dunning G² ≥ 10.83 gate is effectively non-binding at this corpus size, is applied to 30,610 candidates with no multiple-comparison correction, assumes token independence that word counts violate, and is followed by ranking on raw effect size that surfaces the lowest-count, widest-CI words.

**Evidence.** `main.py:1558` `if g2 < 10.83: continue`, then `:1564-1567` sorts by `log_ratio` and takes `[:30]`. Recomputing the endpoint's own query: 30,610 candidates pass the pos/length/min_count=80 filters; **27,068 (88.4%)** clear G² ≥ 10.83. Expected false positives at uncorrected p<0.001 over 30,610 tests ≈ 31; no BH/Bonferroni is applied in `explore_compare` (contrast `main.py:1380`, where BH *is* applied to trends). Corpus sizes fed to G² are tp = 1,051,965,364 and tn = 836,559,376 tokens summed from `frequency_timeseries`, inflated ~5–6x by the multi-lemma credit defect (F2/F4); deflating all four cells by 5.5x drops the pass rate to 73.2%. ### 3.2 Diachronic embedding methodology

#### F12 (high) — Drift trajectories are step functions: ~96% of reported drift is time-invariant model noise · *1/2*

**Claim.** `drift_score` — the number ranked in every discovery list and printed on every word page — is dominated by a constant independent-training offset.

**Evidence.** `SELECT corpus, year, AVG(distance_from_ref) FROM diachronic_trajectory GROUP BY corpus, year` — news: 2011 = 0.0000, 2013 = 0.1171, 2016 = 0.2809, 2017 = 0.2813, 2018 = 0.2785, 2019 = 0.2827, 2020 = 0.2840, 2021 = 0.2850, 2022 = 0.2875, 2023 = 0.2904, 2024 = 0.2914. The entire 2016→2024 evolution is **+0.0105 = 3.6%** of the final value. Parliament: 1989 = 0.0, 1990 = 0.1492, 1994 = 0.2541, 1996 = 0.2692, 2020 = 0.3157 — 85.3% of the level established in the first ~5 years. The code excludes this floor from one estimator but not the other: `change_point_year` skips the reference→first step because "it's all model variance" (`ingest_diachronic.py:220-221`), while `drift = _cos_dist(v_first, aligned_last)` at `ingest_diachronic.py:548` keeps it in full. Direct measurement of the floor: under ±2-year pooling, `_pool_members(2011, [2011,2013,2016..2024], 2)` and `_pool_members(2013, …, 2)` **both return `[2011, 2013]`** (`ingest_diachronic.py:152-154`) — the two models are trained on the same population, and their measured mean distance is 0.1171. At the observed plateau slope (0.0013/yr) that same-data floor is worth ~89 years of "semantic change".

**Why it matters.** The dynamic range of the published quantity is ~4% signal and ~96% estimation offset, so word-to-word differences are differences in vector-estimation stability (i.e. frequency — see F13), not meaning. The UI claim «μεγαλύτερη απόσταση = μεγαλύτερη αλλαγή στα συμφραζόμενα» (`MethodologyPage.tsx:70-72`) is not supported by the data the pipeline produced. The badge is rendered at `DiachronicPanel.tsx:202`.

**Fix.** Store and subtract the same-slice control distance (`bootstrap_drift.py` already computes it) and publish `drift_excess = max(0, drift − ctrl_p97.5)`; or switch the headline to the plateau **slope** (OLS over `distance_from_ref` across comparison slices), which is free of the constant offset. Render the trajectory with the reference point removed and the y-axis starting at the control floor.

#### F5 / F9 (critical) — `change_point_year` is a corpus-gap artifact and the advertised z≥2 gate was never applied · *2/2*

**Claim.** Stored change points are overwhelmingly the first slice after a hole in year coverage, and the confidence gate the Methodology page promises is absent from 100% of shipped rows.

**Evidence.** `SELECT corpus, change_point_year, COUNT(*) ... GROUP BY 1,2` → news 2016 = **9,764 of 11,825 (82.6%)**; next-largest year 2017 at 469. The materialized news slices are 2011, 2013, 2016, 2017…2024 (`ls data/processed/diachronic/news`) — **2012, 2014 and 2015 are missing**, so the 2013→2016 step spans a 3-year hole and is mechanically the largest step in almost every series (mean trajectory jumps 0.1171→0.2809 there vs ±0.003 per step afterwards). Parliament repeats this at its 1995 hole: 1994 (1,300) + 1996 (1,021) + 1993 (593) = 34% of all parliament change points. Coverage is near-total: 11,825/11,835 news (99.9%) and 8,430/8,430 parliament (100%). `MIN/MAX(change_point_score)` returns NULL/NULL for both corpora — NULL in all 20,265 rows, so the shipped values predate the #44 suppression logic (`ingest_diachronic.py:240-243`). `MethodologyPage.tsx:79-83` nevertheless tells users the change point is shown «μόνο όταν εκείνο το βήμα ξεχωρίζει στατιστικά … (ανθεκτικό z ≥ 2)», and `DiachronicPanel.tsx:228-233` renders «καμπή ~{year}» whenever the year is non-null, falling back to a generic tooltip when the score is NULL.

**Why it matters.** The product tells a Greek speaker that 8 of every 10 words in the news corpus underwent a statistically distinguishable meaning shift in 2016. That is a statement about Leipzig's download availability, not about Greek.

**Fix.** Re-run ingest so `change_point_score` is populated and the z≥2 gate fires; additionally suppress any change point whose entering step spans a gap in the materialized year list (`|year − previous_year| > 1`). Until then, stop rendering change points.

#### F13 (high) — The Dubossarsky frequency confound is uncontrolled; both "frequency-robust" measures are equally confounded · *1/2*

**Claim.** Drift magnitude is strongly anti-correlated with corpus frequency, the `min_pm` gate advertised as the Dubossarsky control removes almost nothing, and the Gonen neighbour-overlap measure labelled "frequency-robust" is just as confounded and largely redundant with cosine drift.

**Evidence.** Correlating stored drift against corpus-*internal* frequency (mean `per_million` from `frequency_timeseries`, same corpus): Spearman = **−0.547** (news, n=11,835) and **−0.557** (parliament, n=8,430). Frequency deciles, mean drift, news: 0.399 / 0.368 / 0.341 / 0.331 / 0.308 / 0.284 / 0.262 / 0.235 / 0.204 / 0.184 — the rarest decile drifts **2.2x** the commonest; parliament 0.455 → 0.186 = 2.4x. The gate does not fix it: at the API default `min_pm=1.0` (`main.py:1000, 1100`) only 158 of 11,835 news lemmas are dropped (1.3%) and ρ stays at −0.544; even at `min_pm=20` (half the vocabulary discarded) ρ is −0.382 and the top-20 movers still average drift 0.738. `neighbor_overlap`, described as the frequency-robust alternative at `main.py:1062` («neighbor-overlap drift — Gonen et al. 2020 (frequency-robust, interpretable)»), `main.py:1013` and `ExplorePage.tsx:257` («Δύο μέτρα ανθεκτικά στη συχνότητα»), has Spearman(overlap, log per_million) = **−0.522** (news) / −0.501 (parliament) — indistinguishable from cosine drift's confound — and Spearman(overlap, drift) = **0.794** (news) / 0.758 (parliament). Nothing residualizes, bins, or frequency-matches; the only frequency parameter in the training path is `min_count=10` (`ingest_diachronic.py:296`).

**Why it matters.** ~30% of the rank variance in the headline drift number is explained by frequency alone, so "biggest movers" is substantially a list of the rarest words that survived the gate (mean drift 0.88 at the default gate). Presenting two measures side by side as mutually corroborating "frequency-robust" evidence is a false independence claim — they agree because they share the artifact.

**Fix.** Regress drift on log frequency within corpus and rank on the residual, or rank within frequency-matched strata (the Dubossarsky design). Remove the "frequency-robust" label at `main.py:1013/1062` and `ExplorePage.tsx:257` until it is earned, and publish the measured ρ for whatever measure ships.

#### F14 (high) — Procrustes is not Hamilton-compliant, and anchor pruning prunes by vector norm, not drift · *1/2*

**Claim.** `_align` fits the rotation on raw, uncentered, unnormalized word2vec vectors — Hamilton et al. 2016 normalize before alignment — and the iterative "drop the drifted anchors" refinement that the Methodology page presents as the key correctness guarantee actually selects on vector magnitude.

**Evidence.** `ingest_diachronic.py:265-267` stacks vectors straight from gensim (`A = np.vstack([other_wv[w] for w in anchors])`) and calls `orthogonal_procrustes(A, B)` with no mean-centering and no L2 normalization; the docstring at `:1-7` and `MethodologyPage.tsx:65-66` both cite Hamilton et al. 2016, whose procedure normalizes. The prune criterion at `:272` is `resid = np.linalg.norm(A @ R - B, axis=1)` — an **absolute Euclidean residual**, which scales with vector norm, while the reported quantity (`_cos_dist`, `:206-213`) is scale-free. Simulation (d=100, n=5000, lognormal norms σ=0.6, an exact orthogonal Q, and an *identical* angular perturbation of 0.15 rad on every word so no word drifts more than any other): **corr(residual, norm) = 0.996**; the two prune iterations at `:269-278` discard 1,250 then 938 anchors whose mean norms are 6.10 and 3.46 vs 2.30 and 1.91 for the kept set; the surviving anchor set spans only the 0th–58th norm percentile. Because word2vec norm grows with training count, the refinement systematically evicts the highest-frequency, best-estimated words — the exact opposite of the stated rationale («η περιστροφή υπολογίζεται μόνο πάνω σε σταθερές, υψίσυχνες λέξεις», `MethodologyPage.tsx:66-67`). `tests/test_anchored_align.py` cannot detect this: `_build` at line 40 draws all vectors from `rng.normal(size=(n,d))`, so every vector has norm ≈ √d and the confound is absent by construction. Separately, `_train` uses `sg=0` (CBOW, `ingest_diachronic.py:139`) while Hamilton 2016 uses SGNS and the sibling `classify_domains.py` uses skip-gram — the two embedding layers of the same project are not built the same way.

**Why it matters.** The alignment frame is fitted on a norm-selected, frequency-anti-selected subset of ~56% of candidate anchors, in a space that was never normalized. Every drift number is measured against that frame, and the unit test certifying it is blind to the failure mode.

**Fix.** Mean-center and L2-normalize both matrices before `orthogonal_procrustes` (Hamilton 2016 §3.1); switch the prune criterion at `:272` to a scale-free residual (cosine between `A@R` rows and `B` rows). Add a regression test giving anchors heterogeneous norms (multiply rows by `exp(N(0,0.6))`) and asserting the pruned set is not norm-selected.

#### F15 (high) — ±2-year pooling weighted by raw line count decouples a slice's label from its content · *2/2*

**Claim.** Pooled slices are built by uniform reservoir sampling over concatenated member-year files, so the mixture is weighted by each year's raw sentence count; with the news corpus's 300k→1M size jump at 2019, the model **labelled 2018 is mostly 2019–2020 text**, two news slices are trained on identical populations, and the induced overlap is never accounted for downstream.

**Evidence.** `ingest_diachronic.py:172-203` `_pooled_to_tempfile` reservoir-samples uniformly over lines from all member files. Measured slice sizes (line counts of `data/processed/diachronic/news/*.txt`): 2011 = 299,997; 2013 = 300,000; 2016–2018 = 300,000 each; **2019–2024 = 1,000,000 each** — a 3.3x discontinuity at 2019, i.e. the news axis is two different corpora glued together. With `pool_window=2`: `_pool_members(2018, …)` = [2016,2017,2018,2019,2020] = 0.3M+0.3M+0.3M+1M+1M, so **69% of the training text for the slice labelled 2018 comes from 2019–2020** and its size-weighted mean content year is 2018.72; for the slice labelled 2017 (members 2016–2019) it is 2018.05 — a **+1.05-year label error**. Worse, `_pool_members(2011, …, 2)` and `_pool_members(2013, …, 2)` both return `[2011, 2013]` (2012 absent), so those two "independent time slices" are drawn from one identical population, and their measured distance (0.1171) is a pure noise reading. Adjacent slices otherwise share 60–100% of their members. Nothing corrects for this: the change-point detector builds its noise baseline from the median/MAD of those same overlapping steps (`:224, 233-240`), deflating σ and inflating z; the trends OLS and the bootstrap CI likewise treat slices as independent. The pool cap (`--pool-cap` 500,000, `:300`) binds on every news slice but on **no** parliament slice (largest band ≈ 260k lines), so the two axes use different effective estimators.

**Why it matters.** A trajectory point labelled 2018 that is 69% 2019–2020 text destroys the temporal resolution the chart claims, and shifts any inferred change point by up to a year in a corpus-size-dependent direction. The 2011/2013 duplication makes the first visible step on every news word's trajectory definitionally zero-signal.

**Fix.** Weight pooled members equally (sample `cap/len(members)` lines per member year) so the label year is the centroid of content, and record the size-weighted mean year alongside each slice. Refuse to emit two slices whose member sets are identical. Apply the cap identically across corpora, and either drop the change-point z or compute its baseline from non-overlapping slice pairs.

#### F44 (medium) — No diachronic result is reproducible: word2vec trained with `workers=cpu_count()` and no seed · *1/2*

**Evidence.** `ingest_diachronic.py:131-141`: `Word2Vec(corpus_file=path, vector_size=…, window=…, min_count=…, workers=os.cpu_count() or 4, sg=0, epochs=epochs)` — **no `seed=` argument** and workers>1, which makes gensim's asynchronous updates order-dependent even with a fixed seed. The only seeding in the module is `random.seed(seed)` at `:310`, commented «makes the pooled-reservoir sampling reproducible», with the CLI help at `:626` documenting exactly that limited scope. `bootstrap_drift.py:52-55` imports and reuses this `_train`, so the bootstrap replicates inherit the nondeterminism — the "no-change control" is measuring thread-scheduling variance as well as resampling variance, without separating them. There is no run manifest, no config hash and no version column on `diachronic_drift`/`diachronic_trajectory`.

**Why it matters.** An open-data project publishing per-word semantic-change numbers cannot have them re-derived by anyone, including its maintainers. The observed 0.117 same-data floor and the control band are partly artifacts of thread scheduling that a reader cannot bound.

**Fix.** Pass an explicit `seed=` and set `workers=1` for the released build (or publish run-to-run variance over ≥5 identical reruns). Persist a provenance row per corpus (git SHA, all CLI args, gensim/numpy versions, slice line counts).

**Claim.** The classifier is trained and scored exclusively on lemmas Wiktionary already tagged with a subject field, but applied to every content lemma with a vector; it has no "no domain" option, so its largest deployment error mode is structurally invisible to every reported metric.

#### F17 (high) — `class_weight='balanced'` + one absolute softmax gate: 0.55 means posterior 0.076 for economics and 0.881 for medicine · *1/2*

**Evidence.** `classify_domains.py:195` and `:213` both pass `class_weight="balanced"`; the gate at `:258` and the API display gate at `main.py:1188` (`_DOMAIN_PRED_GATE = 0.55`, applied `:1213`) are single absolute constants. **Fix.** Drop `class_weight='balanced'` for the production refit (keep it only for reported macro metrics), or apply an explicit prior correction `p_true ∝ p_bal · π_c` before gating, or set per-field thresholds from a measured per-field precision curve. #### F45 (medium) — Reported metrics describe a different model under a different decision rule · *1/2*

**Evidence.** `classify_domains.py:194-197` fits `clf_eval` on Xtr only; `:198` `pred_te = clf_eval.predict(Xte)` is a bare argmax — `CONF_FLOOR` and `MARGIN_FLOOR` (`:69-70`) are never applied to the evaluation. `:213-214` fits a *different* estimator `clf` on all X, and `:248-266` applies conf≥0.50 **and** margin≥0.10 before writing. The API applies a third, independent gate (`main.py:1188/1213`, 0.55). The returned report (`:278-292`) contains `held_out_precision_by_field` labelled without mention of any gate, plus `"calibration": "none"`. `precision_score(..., zero_division=0)` at `:204` reports 0.000 for a never-predicted class rather than undefined, and any class absent from `yte` is silently dropped.

**Why it matters.** Gating should raise precision, so 0.56 may understate precision at 0.55 — or, given F17, overstate it for rare fields. Nobody knows which, and the number published to users is measured on a decision rule never used in production.

**Fix.** Report metrics from `clf_eval` **with the production gate applied** (precision/coverage at 0.30/0.50/0.55/0.70 on the held-out split, per field) and report the abstention rate. Make coverage-vs-precision the headline pair.

#### F46 (medium) — Single 20% split, no CV, no CI, ~4 test examples in the smallest class — and the same split chose the architecture · *1/2*

**Evidence.** `classify_domains.py:191-193` single `train_test_split(test_size=0.2, random_state=seed)`, no KFold/`cross_val_score` anywhere; no bootstrap or CI on acc/macro_f1 (`:199-206`, `:278-292`). n_test = 0.2 × 4,983 ≈ 997 → binomial SE on accuracy 0.0157, 95% CI [0.529, 0.591]. Estimated per-class test counts (candidate counts × 0.403 vector retention × 0.2): economics **3.9**, sports 13.4, military 18.6, philosophy 22.5, technology 28.8, mathematics 30.8 vs medicine 261.0. With 4 test examples the economics F1 can take only 5 values (0.000, 0.034, 0.068, 0.102, 0.136 at ~55 predicted). A binomial simulation of macro-F1 at these class sizes (20,000 draws, per-class recall 0.46) gives sd 0.016 and a 95% range of width 0.064 from sampling noise alone. `HANDOFF.md:583-585` records that skip-gram was chosen because held-out acc went 0.478→0.560 and macro-F1 0.383→0.463 — i.e. the reported test set is a model-selection set. `C=4.0` (`:195`, `:213`) is a non-default with no stated derivation.

#### F2 (critical) — Zipf values are arithmetically impossible: per-lemma counts sum to 1,026% of the corpus · *2/2*

**Claim.** The Zipf formula matches van Heuven et al. (2014), but its numerator credits each surface's full count to every `search_index` **row** that matches — not every distinct lemma — so a lemma is credited repeatedly for duplicate index rows.

**Evidence.** `ingest_frequency.py:104` `zipf = math.log10(count) - math.log10(total) + 9.0` = log10(count/total·1e9) — formula correct. But `:86-88` builds `surface_to_lemmas.setdefault(norm, []).append(lemma_id)` (a **list**, not a set) and `:97-98` does `for lid in ids: lemma_count[lid] += count`. Shipped DB: `SUM(frequency.count) = 2,584,088,301` against a corpus total of 251,785,885 (measured sum of `data/raw/el_freq.txt`) = **1,026%**. A faithful re-simulation against the live index reproduces the top of the table exactly (ο 103,657,620; αυτός 75,202,407; τα 70,805,514; του 42,689,528; χυδαίος 41,828,964) and yields 2,699,976,810 credited tokens, of which **907,217,101 (33.6%)** are credited to a lemma that had *already* counted that surface — pure duplicate index rows, not homograph ambiguity. Mean index rows per matched surface = 10.93; mean distinct lemmas per matched surface = 1.52 (max 112); 31.1% of matched surfaces map to >1 lemma and carry 72.5% of matched tokens. Per-lemma duplicate-only inflation: median **1.286x**, mean 2.556x, 46.0% of the 25,945 scored lemmas ≥2x, p90 5.05x, max **21.09x** (≈ +1.32 Zipf). **225 lemmas have Zipf > 7** (>1% of the corpus each), 714 above 6; `MAX(zipf) = 8.615`.

**Why it matters.** Zipf is presented as a calibrated cross-linguistic scale (`WordPage.tsx:32` shows "Zipf X.X" plus a 5-bar meter). 8.6 is off the scale van Heuven defines, and the error is not a constant offset — it varies from 1x to 21x with how many index rows a lemma owns, so band assignment and the `frequency.rank` ORDER BY driving `/api/search` are both wrong in a word-dependent way. Any comparison against SUBTLEX/wordfreq is invalid.

**Fix.** Deduplicate to a set before crediting (`for lid in set(ids)`), and add `UNIQUE(lemma_id, surface, normalized_surface)` on `search_index`. Then decide the ambiguity policy explicitly: split a shared surface's count across candidates, or mark such lemmas frequency-unreliable. Re-derive Zipf against the true corpus token total, and assert in a test that `Σ 10^(zipf−9) ≤ 1.05`.

### 3.6 Modern Greek linguistic correctness

#### F3 (critical) — el-wiktionary verb forms carry no voice tag; active and mediopassive merge into one grid · *critic*

**Evidence.** `select count(*) from forms f join lemmas l on l.id=f.lemma_id where l.pos='verb' and f.source='el-wiktionary' and (features like '%passive%' or features like '%active%')` = **6,470 of 635,350 (1.0%)**. σκοτώνω stores **both** `('έχω σκοτώσει', '["perfect","first-person","singular"]')` and `('έχω σκοτωθεί', '["perfect","first-person","singular"]')` — byte-identical feature lists. `inflection.ts:334-336` partitions solely on `f.features.some(x => x.toLowerCase() === "passive")`, so both land in `active`; `:184-185` pushes both into `cellMap` for key `1s|perf` and `:216` joins them with `" / "`. #### F40 (high) — `greek_accent` is blind to polytonic and capital accents; the engine adds a second accent · *critic*

## 4. Literature review

Eight areas, each connecting the published standard to specific Lexorama decisions.

### 4.1 Diachronic distributional semantics / lexical semantic change (LSC) detection

**Verdict: Marginal.** The method design is broadly literature-aligned and unusually self-aware in code comments, but the shipped database does not implement the methodology the code and the user-facing methodology page claim, and the headline outputs (change points, "biggest movers") are dominated by measurable alignment/estimation artifacts rather than semantic change.

**1. How the paradigm was built (2014–2016).** The alignment paradigm Lexorama implements has a short, well-documented lineage. Kim, Chiu, Hanaki, Hegde & Petrov (ACL 2014 LTCSS workshop, 61–65) trained a neural LM per year on Google Books and side-stepped comparability by *initialising* each year's model from the previous — continuous training, no post-hoc alignment. Kulkarni, Al-Rfou, Perozzi & Skiena (WWW 2015, 625–635) introduced two ideas that matter here: linear-transformation alignment of independently trained spaces, and — crucially — treating change detection as *change-point detection over a time series* with an explicit significance procedure (bootstrapped null distributions and p-values), rather than reporting the largest raw distance. Hamilton, Leskovec & Jurafsky (ACL 2016, 1489–1501) canonicalised orthogonal Procrustes (OP) alignment across PPMI/SVD/SGNS spaces, released HistWords, and proposed the "law of conformity" (change rate ∝ frequency⁻¹) and "law of innovation" (polysemy ↑ change).

**2. The counter-literature, which is the important part.** Within a year the laws were shown to be largely artifacts. Dubossarsky, Weinshall & Grossman ("Outta Control", EMNLP 2017, 1136–1145) introduced the device that is now the field's default hygiene requirement: a *control condition* in which no change can have occurred (e.g. random splits of the same time period). They showed the conformity and prototypicality laws replicate almost as strongly in the control as in the genuine condition — i.e. the "laws" mostly measure how vector estimation error scales with frequency, not language change. The lesson generalises: **any** cosine-drift number must be read against a matched no-change baseline of the same corpus size and word frequency.

Dubossarsky, Hengchen, Tahmasebi & Schlechtweg ("Time-Out", ACL 2019, 457–470) attacked the alignment step directly: SOTA LSC models "suffer from noise stemming from vector space alignment", and Temporal Referencing — training one model on the concatenated corpus with target words time-indexed (`crisis_1989`, `crisis_2020`) — outperforms OP-aligned SGNS on synthetic and manual tests precisely because there is nothing to align. In parallel Antoniak & Mimno (TACL 6, 2018, 107–119) showed nearest-neighbour structure is highly unstable under small corpus perturbations and recommended, in terms, that users **never rely on a single embedding model** but average over bootstrap resamples of *documents*. Shoemark, Liza, Nguyen, Hale & McGillivray ("Room to Glo", EMNLP-IJCNLP 2019, D19-1007) built a synthetic-change evaluation and reported three findings of immediate relevance: (i) using the **whole time series** beats first-vs-last comparison; (ii) independently-trained-and-aligned embeddings beat continuously-trained ones over long spans; (iii) **the reference point matters** — results are not invariant to which slice you align everything to.

**3. Alternatives to alignment.** Bamler & Mandt ("Dynamic Word Embeddings", ICML 2017, PMLR 70:380–389) and Rudolph & Blei (WWW 2018, 1003–1011) replace post-hoc alignment with a joint probabilistic model: embeddings are latent trajectories tied across time by a diffusion / dynamic exponential-family prior, so the spaces are comparable by construction and low-count slices are regularised by their neighbours. Gonen, Jawahar, Seddah & Goldberg (ACL 2020, 538–555) go the other way: drop alignment entirely and compare a word's top-k nearest-neighbour *sets* across corpora, arguing this is simpler, interpretable and more stable, validated over 9 corpus-splitting setups in English, French and Hebrew. Contextualised approaches — Martinc, Kralj Novak & Pollak (LREC 2020, 4811–4819) and Giulianelli, Del Tredici & Fernández (ACL 2020, 3960–3973) — measure change over token-level usage distributions.

**4. Evaluation without ground truth.** The community converged on human judgement over *usage pairs*. DURel-style annotation feeds graded and binary gold standards; SemEval-2020 Task 1 (Schlechtweg, McGillivray, Hengchen, Dubossarsky & Tahmasebi, 1–23) is the standard. Concrete numbers: English (CCOHA, 37 targets), German (48), Latin (40), Swedish (31); 33 teams, 186 systems. Subtask 1 (binary) best average accuracy **.687** (UWB), against majority-class **.576**, count/column-intersection **.613**, frequency-difference **.439**. Subtask 2 (graded ranking) best average Spearman **.527**; count baseline **.144**. Two organiser conclusions are load-bearing: (a) **7 of the top 10 Subtask-1 systems and 8 of the top 10 Subtask-2 systems used type embeddings**, so SGNS+OP or SGNS+TR was the strong recipe in 2020; (b) strong **frequency biases persisted in individual models** (correlations >0.8 with frequency metrics on deliberately frequency-controlled targets). Note the absolute scale: the *winner* clears a trivial baseline by 11 accuracy points and reaches ρ≈0.53. Anyone shipping per-word drift numbers to end users ships a signal whose best-known validated ceiling is a moderate rank correlation. Cassotti et al.'s XL-LEXEME (ACL 2023 short, 1577–1585) has since moved the frontier, but requires WiC supervision Lexorama does not have.

**5. Is Procrustes-to-a-single-reference still sound?** Qualified yes, as a *baseline*, with three riders the field treats as mandatory: it must be paired with a no-change control (Dubossarsky 2017); it should not be endpoint-only (Shoemark 2019); and the reference slice is a free parameter that biases results, so either report sensitivity to it, use temporal referencing, or use an alignment-free measure. Schlechtweg, Hätty, del Tredici & Schulte im Walde (ACL 2019, 732–746) found no single dominant alignment configuration. On uncertainty: the standard bootstrap literature puts **B ≈ 25–200 for standard errors but B ≥ 1,000–2,000 for percentile confidence intervals**; Antoniak & Mimno's embedding-specific recommendation is to bootstrap the *documents*. K=12 does not produce a defensible 95% interval; K=100 gives percentile cut-points that are still visibly granular.

**Gaps vs Lexorama.**

*Where Lexorama is genuinely defensible.* Per-slice word2vec + OP alignment (`ingest_diachronic.py:246-283`) is the SemEval-validated family; the full trajectory rather than endpoints (`:461-504`) is exactly Shoemark's finding (i); Gonen 2020 neighbour-overlap is implemented alongside cosine (`:519-541`) with the `restrict_vocab` fix the setup needs; a Dubossarsky-style no-change control exists (`bootstrap_drift.py:278-287`); BH-FDR over thousands of simultaneous tests (`:56-73`) is more than most published papers do; and the "cardinal rule" of one corpus per axis (`ingest_diachronic.py:14-17`) correctly refuses to let Procrustes align register differences. The anchored/iteratively-pruned Procrustes (`:266-278`) is a reasonable idea but is **not a published method** — an unvalidated local invention presented on `MethodologyPage.tsx:66-70` as if standard (and see F14: it prunes by norm).

*Docs/code mismatch on the significance rule.* The stated invariant and `MethodologyPage.tsx:95-97` say significance is CI non-overlap (`real_pct2.5 > ctrl_pct97.5`). The code does something materially different and more liberal: `bootstrap_drift.py:305-315` computes `z = (mean_real − mean_ctrl)/sd_ctrl` and a one-sided **normal** survival function, then BH-FDR. The normal approximation on a bounded cosine distance with a very small `sd_ctrl` inflates z arbitrarily, and the control samples are **not independent**: `:279-281` draws up to K pairs from only K underlying first-slice models, so each model appears in ~2 pairs and `sd_ctrl` (ddof=1) understates the true null spread. Result: **449/479 (93.7%)** of tested parliament lemmas and **621/752 (82.6%)** of news lemmas are "significant". A test that passes >80% of everything is not discriminating.

*The shipped database does not match the shipped code.* `change_point_score` NULL for all 20,265 rows (`:500-504` writes it unconditionally); the z≥2 suppression `MethodologyPage.tsx:80-83` promises never fired (`change_point_year` non-NULL for 99.9%/100%); `drift_score_boot` NULL for all rows so `main.py:953-954` silently falls back; CIs exist for only 776/11,835 news (6.6%) and 490/8,430 parliament (5.8%) versus HANDOFF's claimed 9,003 and 9,225.

*The point estimate falls outside its own CI ~1/3 of the time.* 260/776 (33.5%) news and 170/490 (34.7%) parliament rows have `drift_score` outside `[ci_lo, ci_hi]`; a nominal 95% interval should exclude ~5%. Live example: **σποτ** shows drift 0.7715 with CI [0.4421, 0.6637]. `HANDOFF.md:567-569` claims this was fixed at wave #37; it is not fixed in the data.

*The trajectory is dominated by the alignment noise floor.* See F7/F12. 82.6% of news lemmas share the change point 2016; the change-point step comparison (`:224-243`) treats a 3-year gap as commensurate with 1-year gaps, guaranteeing the wide gap wins.

*Ranking is on the raw point estimate with no significance filter.* `main.py:999-1053` orders by `drift_score DESC` with only a per-million floor. 14 of the top 15 news movers and 15/15 parliament movers have **no CI at all**.

*No lemmatization — a Greek-specific gap the SemEval standard does not have.* `corpus_sources.py:46-55` only lowercases, folds final sigma and strips edge punctuation; vectors are keyed on **inflected surface forms**. All four SemEval-2020 corpora were distributed lemmatized. In a language where a noun has 8+ surface forms and a verb dozens, each type's counts are fragmented, `min_count=10` bites much harder, and νόμος/νόμο/νόμου are three unrelated vectors while the drift score attaches to the citation form only. This alone plausibly explains a large share of the estimation noise.

*CBOW, not skip-gram.* `ingest_diachronic.py:131-141` trains with `sg=0`, dim 100, 5 epochs. The LSC standard is SGNS. The project's own `classify_domains.py` documents that skip-gram beat CBOW substantially on this data (0.478→0.560), making the CBOW choice inconsistent with its own evidence.

*What is missing entirely.* No temporal-referencing variant to check alignment sensitivity; no sensitivity analysis over the reference slice; no synthetic-change validation and **no human-annotated Greek gold standard of any size** — so there is currently zero empirical evidence that any Lexorama drift number correlates with a Greek speaker's judgement. `neighbor_overlap` averages 0.80 (news) / 0.785 (parliament) with 175/172 lemmas at exactly 1.0, i.e. near-saturated on a 0–1 scale where 1 means "completely different company".

**Minimum defensible fixes, in priority order:** (1) re-run the methodology waves to completion, or remove the z-gate and CI claims from `MethodologyPage.tsx`; (2) suppress the change point entirely on the noise ramp; (3) do not rank by an estimate with no interval; (4) switch to SGNS and lemmatize (or aggregate surface forms to lemma before training); (5) reconcile the significance rule in code with the docs.

### 4.2 Corpus-linguistic statistics: keyness, dispersion, and collocation measures

**Verdict: split. Keyness marginal but salvageable; collocations indefensible as currently ranked.**

**1. Corpus frequencies are not random samples.** The modern literature begins with Kilgarriff's demolition of naive significance testing. In "Comparing corpora" (*IJCL* 6(1):97–133, 2001) he split the written BNC into two random halves of the *same* texts (820 texts >20k words), so by construction there was no linguistic difference to find. Under the χ² null the mean error term should be 0.5. He measured **18.76** for the ten most frequent words, 17.45, 14.39, 10.71, 7.03 … falling to 1.15 only for the rarest 40,960-word band — against a per-cell critical value of 3.94. The null was defeated for most words in two corpora that were the same corpus. "Language is never, ever, ever, random" (*CLLT* 1(2):263–276, 2005) generalises: the randomness null is definitionally false, so p-values measure corpus size, not linguistic interest. Gries's reply (*CLLT* 1(2):277–294) accepts the diagnosis while defending NHST as a *filter*. Settled consensus: **significance may gate, but must never rank**.

**2. Effect size and its opposite pathology.** LogRatio (Hardie, CASS blog, 2014), %DIFF (Gabrielatos & Marchi 2012) and the odds ratio cure the high-frequency bias and introduce a low-frequency one. Gabrielatos's typology chapter (in Taylor & Marchi, *Corpus Approaches to Discourse*, Routledge 2018:225–258) is the standard taxonomy. Evert's "Measuring Keyness" (DH2022, 202–205) is the sharpest statement of where this leaves us: LogRatio's low-frequency bias is *"hardly mitigated"* by adding a G² filter — the filter changes which items survive, not what the ranking rewards; and multiple testing is routinely ignored, since "a single analysis may carry out frequency comparisons for hundreds of thousands of candidates, resulting in large numbers of false positives at customary significance levels such as p < .001". Her prescription, **LRC**, is concrete and cheap: compute a two-sided Clopper–Pearson interval for f₁ of f₁+f₂ at Bonferroni-adjusted α = 0.05/m, convert both endpoints to the log₂ relative-frequency scale, set LRC = 0 if the interval straddles zero, else take the bound nearer zero. This balances both biases, handles f₂ = 0 without Hardie's unjustified f₂ ← 0.5 patch, supplies the significance filter, corrects for multiplicity, and removes arbitrary frequency cut-offs. **Ranking by the conservative bound rather than the point estimate is the single highest-leverage correction available to any keyness implementation.**

**3. Dispersion.** Gries (*IJCL* 13(4):403–437, 2008) proposed DP = ½·Σ|vᵢ − sᵢ|. Lijffijt & Gries (*IJCL* 17(1):147–149, 2012) corrected the normalisation: DP_norm = DP/(1 − 1/n) is valid only for equal parts; for unequal parts **DP_norm = DP/(1 − min(sᵢ))**. Biber, Reppen, Schnur & Ghanem (*IJCL* 21(4):439–464, 2016) showed Juilland's D is unusable at scale: the same uniform distribution over 10% of a corpus yields D = 0.0 at n = 10 parts and D = 0.905 at n = 1000. Gries's handbook chapter (Springer 2020:99–118) systematises this; Gries (*JSLS* 5(2):171–205, 2022) argues most dispersion measures are "some amalgam of a lot of frequency and a little dispersion". The crucial design point: **the parts must be many and comparably sized, ideally texts/documents**. Egbert & Biber (*Corpora* 14(1):77–104, 2019) fused this with keyness — compute LL on *text ranges* rather than corpus frequencies — and found that corpus-frequency measures patched with a minimum text-range criterion "performed quite poorly on all of the metrics", i.e. bolting a dispersion gate onto a frequency measure is not equivalent to a dispersion-based measure. Gries (*RiCL* 9(2):1–33, 2021) counters that dispersion should *augment* rather than replace frequency, and proposes a two-dimensional KL-divergence keyness, showing that *about* and *hillaryclinton* receive near-identical LLR although one is a 1.6× ratio present in every speech and the other is used by one speaker only.

**4. Collocation: log-likelihood is the wrong default.** Church & Hanks (*CL* 16(1):22–29, 1990) introduced PMI, which has a severe low-frequency bias; Evert ("Corpora and collocations", 2008) recommends f ≥ 5 as the minimal defence. Dunning's G² (*CL* 19(1):61–74, 1993) is the best *significance* measure but has an explicit **high-frequency bias**: "if O is sufficiently large, even a small relative difference … can be highly significant". Evert's worked *bucket* example gives simple-ll's top collocates as *water, a, spade, plastic, size, slop, mop, throw, fill, with* — function words and generic nouns. Rychlý (RASLAN 2008) makes the same point with BNC *break*: T-score returns *the, ., ,, be, and, to, a, of*; MI returns *spell-wall (f=5), deadlock, hoodoo (f=3), scapulum (f=3)*; Dice returns *down, silence, leg, law, heart, rule, off, news*. He therefore defines **logDice = 14 + log₂(2·f_xy/(f_x+f_y))**, theoretical max 14 (usually <10), +1 point = twice as frequent, +7 ≈ 100×, and — decisively for a dictionary — **independent of corpus size and stable on subcorpora**. Gablasova, Brezina & McEnery (*Language Learning* 67(S1):155–179, 2017) add that studies must report the measure, window span and frequency threshold.

**Synthesis for a dictionary.** Collocations: logDice (or log odds ratio) as the ranking measure, an explicit f_xy floor (≥5), significance only as a gate, and syntactic or narrow-window co-occurrence over whole-sentence. Keyness: rank by a dispersion-aware, multiplicity-corrected conservative effect size, computed over many document-level parts, treating p-values as filters at best.

**Gaps vs Lexorama.** See F19 and F20 for the measured detail. In summary: (a) `ingest_collocations.py:136-154` ranks by Leipzig's Dunning LL — the measure the literature names as the wrong default — while `read_words_file` (`:69-78`) and the main loop (`:137-148`) discard f_x, f_y and f_xy, the three numbers logDice needs, all present in files the pipeline already opens; (b) the entire defence against the high-frequency bias is a ~100-word hand-written stopword list that demonstrably leaks (τις for 1,735 lemmas; 3.58% of rows are function/light words); (c) no f_xy threshold is possible because f_xy is discarded, and `schema.sql:135-143` stores only `score REAL`, so switching measure requires a full re-ingest; (d) whole-sentence co-occurrence with no documented span; (e) `MIN_WORD_LEN = 3` silently deletes 2-letter Greek content words; (f) accent folding in violation of `corpus_sources.py:16-22` (13.84% of collocated lemmas on an ambiguous key; ώμος/ωμός share five of eight collocates; νόμος credited with Μαγνησίας); (g) no methodology disclosure at all — `MethodologyPage.tsx` has a keyness section (`:172-193`) and **no collocation section**, and `WordPage.tsx:453-483` shows chips in score order without the score, measure, corpus, window or threshold. Keyness: the effect-size/significance separation at `main.py:846-871` is right, but ranking is by the point estimate (`:1564-1565`), discarding the CI computed at `:1548` — median displayed CI width 4.02 log₂ (parliament) / 3.63 (news), 29/30 top items have <0.1 pmw in the comparison corpus, and the #1 parliament "distinctive word" is **«τσιπλάκης»**, an MP's surname (LR 9.885, CI [7.056, 12.714], dp 0.748). No multiplicity correction over 30,610 candidates although `_bh_fdr` sits at `main.py:777` and is used for trends and drift. ### 4.3 Bootstrap methods, resampling inference, and multiple comparisons

**Verdict: marginal-to-indefensible for the inference layer as shipped.** The *design* (bootstrap over sentences, Dubossarsky-style control, BH-FDR) is sound and above the norm for a hobby project. Three things break it: (a) the shipped database was produced at K=12, an order of magnitude below the literature standard for percentile intervals; (b) the plain percentile interval currently in the code — the bias correction was **removed** in the last commit — produces intervals that do not contain their own displayed point estimate in ~34% of rows; and (c) `MethodologyPage.tsx` still tells readers that significance is decided by non-overlapping confidence intervals, the specific procedure Schenker & Gentleman (2001) demonstrated is not a valid test.

**1. How many resamples a percentile interval needs.** The consensus splits sharply by target. For a *standard error*, Efron & Tibshirani (1993, §6.4, p. 52) state that "very seldom are more than B = 200 replications needed", and B = 50 already gives a usable estimate. For a *confidence interval* the requirement is an order of magnitude higher, because an interval is a pair of extreme quantiles rather than a second moment; Efron & Tibshirani recommend B between 1000 and 2000. Hall (1986) gives the theoretical reason: the bootstrap's improvement in coverage error from O(n^−1/2) to O(n^−1) holds uniformly in B only when nominal coverage is a multiple of (B+1)^−1 — hence the ubiquitous B = 999 and B = 1999 of Davison & Hinkley (1997), chosen so the empirical quantile is an order statistic rather than an interpolation. Booth & Sarkar (1998) quantify the weaker variance problem: B ≈ 800 to be 95% confident a bootstrap *variance* has relative error below 0.1. Andrews & Buchinsky (2000) formalise the choice with a three-step method targeting a specified percentage-deviation bound. Chernick (2008) reaches the same guidance; nobody in this literature defends double-digit B for an interval.

**2. Monte Carlo error of a bootstrap quantile — the concrete numbers.** The asymptotic SD of the sample p-quantile from B draws is √(p(1−p)/B)/f(q_p). In *probability* units, √(0.025·0.975/B) gives: B=12 → **0.0451**; B=100 → 0.0156; B=1000 → 0.0049; B=2000 → 0.0035. At B=12 the "2.5th percentile" is a ±4.5-percentage-point object — its one-sigma band runs from 0% to ~7%, so the nominal level is not identified to within a factor of three. Worse, the interval degenerates structurally: with B=12 and NumPy's linear interpolation the 2.5% cut-point sits at index 0.025·11 = 0.275, i.e. 27.5% of the way from the smallest to the second-smallest replicate — the interval is essentially the **sample range of 12 draws**. Two exact consequences: the probability that a fresh independent replicate falls inside the range of B previous draws is (B−1)/(B+1) = 11/13 = **84.6%**, so a nominally 95% interval delivers at most ~85% predictive coverage before any bias or skewness; and P(x₍₁₎ < true 2.5% quantile) = 1 − 0.975¹² = **26.2%**, so three times in four the lower endpoint sits *above* the quantity it estimates. Efron (1987) and DiCiccio & Efron (1996) add that the plain percentile interval is only first-order accurate; BCa fixes median bias (z₀) and non-constant variance (acceleration a) and is second-order accurate — but realises its advantage only at large B, since it *shifts* the cut-points and needs finer tail resolution.

**3. Non-overlapping intervals are not a test.** Schenker & Gentleman (2001) is canonical: rejection by overlap implies rejection by the standard method, but not conversely, so the overlap rule is *conservative* and fails to reject far too often. Austin & Hux (2002) make the same point clinically. Cumming & Finch (2005) quantify it: for independent 95% intervals, non-overlap corresponds to roughly p ≤ 0.006, just-touching to p ≈ 0.01, and intervals can overlap by about half of one arm before p reaches 0.05. The arithmetic is transparent — the overlap rule requires the difference to exceed 1.96(se₁+se₂) whereas the correct test requires 1.96·√(se₁²+se₂²); for equal SEs the ratio is √2, so overlap demands an effect ~41% larger and operates at an effective α ≈ 0.005. The correct procedure is to form an interval or test *on the difference itself*.

**4. Multiplicity and dependence.** Benjamini & Hochberg (1995) is the right instrument for thousands of simultaneous lemma-level tests. Two caveats: BH assumes the input p-values are *valid* (uniform under the null) — q-values from mis-calibrated p's inherit the mis-calibration exactly; and under general dependence the guarantee requires Benjamini–Yekutieli (2001) or at least PRDS-type positive dependence, which cross-lemma embedding drift sharing one Procrustes rotation does not obviously satisfy. Applying BH to a set already *selected* on the same data (e.g. filtered by R²) is selective inference and does not control FDR at the nominal level.

**5. Autocorrelated time series.** Newey & West (1987) give the standard HAC covariance estimator. The non-parametric analogue for Mann–Kendall is Hamed & Rao (1998), who derive a corrected variance of S and show positive autocorrelation inflates the probability of detecting trends where none exist. Either fix is cheap on an 11–31-point series; neither is optional if the p-value is displayed.

**Gaps vs Lexorama.** (i) **B two orders of magnitude below standard, and the DB older than the code.** `bootstrap_drift.py:352` now defaults to K=100, but `pipelines/download_data.ps1:47` still documents `--k 12` and `HANDOFF.md:524-535` records production runs at K=12; the K=100 rerun scripted in `pipelines/ingest/_reingest_methodology_waves.sh:34-38` never landed (`drift_score_boot` NULL for 20,265/20,265, written unconditionally by `:326-327`). ### 4.4 Modern Greek NLP resources, morphology, and tooling

**Verdict: split.** (a) The paradigm engine is **defensible-but-marginal**: its 0.99 precision claim reproduces exactly, but it is measured on the wrong population and coverage/recall is never reported, so it is not comparable to any published Greek morphology figure. (b) The form→lemma search layer is **defensible** for its actual purpose (dictionary lookup) but must stop being described in terms that invite comparison with lemmatizers — it is a string index, not an analyzer. (c) The omw-el taxonomy import is **indefensible as shipped**: a sense-blind, word-level projection of sense-level relations, 70% of edges connecting two polysemous lemmas, 452 logically impossible mutual-hypernym pairs, an edge count double-counted ~2x, and a spot-check error rate around 40%.

**What the literature establishes.** Modern Greek is no longer low-resource for core NLP, and the bar is set by public, reproducible numbers. The evaluation substrate is Universal Dependencies: UD_Greek-GDT (Prokopidis & Papageorgiou, UDW@NoDaLiDa 2017) contains 61,673 tokens in 2,521 sentences with **manually annotated lemmas**, licensed CC BY-NC-SA 3.0 (the NC clause propagates); UD_Greek-GUD (Markantonatou, Stamou, Bompolas, Vakirtzian) adds 24,897 tokens in 1,807 sentences, CC BY-SA 4.0, annotated natively in UD style. Against that, CoNLL 2018 gives the canonical form→lemma bar for Greek: on el_gdt, TurkuNLP **97.22** Lemmas-F1, ICS PAS 95.96, Stanford 95.05, UDPipe Future 94.74; the UDPipe 1.2 baseline scored 87.84 macro-averaged and on el_gdt LAS 81.37 / MLAS 63.92 / BLEX 65.21. Off the shelf, spaCy `el_core_news_lg` 3.8.0 reports **LEMMA_ACC 89.83**, POS_ACC 96.41, MORPH_ACC 91.30, UAS 88.09, LAS 84.47. Stanza on UD Greek GDT reports UPOS 97.71, UFeats 94.72, **Lemmas 96.05**, LAS 88.87. So ~95–97% context-resolved lemma accuracy is competitive; ~90% is "usable library default". Two riders matter: those numbers are *contextual* — the system sees a sentence and picks one analysis — so a dictionary mapping an isolated surface string to a candidate set is solving a strictly easier and different problem and cannot borrow them; and GREEK-BERT (Koutsikakis, Chalkidis, Malakasiotis & Androutsopoulos, SETN 2020) and the GR-NLP-TOOLKIT built on it (Loukas et al., COLING 2025 demos) cover POS, morphological tagging, parsing, NER and Greeklish transliteration but **not lemmatization** — the strongest Greek neural toolkit deliberately declines the task.

**Rule-based vs FST vs neural.** The relevant question is not "rules or neural" but "is your morphology lexicon-conditioned?" Koskenniemi's two-level model (1983) and its successors — PC-KIMMO, then foma (Hulden, EACL 2009 demos) and HFST — encode inflection as a lexicon of stems each tagged with a continuation class, composed with parallel rewrite rules. Greek has been done this way: Sgarbas, Fakotakis & Kokkinakis published a PC-KIMMO two-level description of Modern Greek (*LLC* 10(3), 1995) over 60,000+ lemmata, and later a DAWG-based full-form approach (COMLEX 2000, arXiv:cs/0112010). The decisive property of an FST is that lexically idiosyncratic behaviour — whether *άνθρωπος* takes learned *ανθρώπου*; whether *-η* feminines pluralise *-ες* or ancient *-εις* — is stored **per stem**, not inferred from the ending. A pure ending-driven generator cannot represent that and must guess or abstain. Neural seq2seq inflection exceeds 90% on most languages but requires held-out-*lemma* evaluation and gives no coverage guarantee; the Estonian case study (Dorkin & Sirts, NoDaLiDa 2023) finds generative character models beat pattern-classification models and that rule-based and neural errors barely overlap — the empirical case for hybrids. For a dictionary whose contract is "never show a wrong form", an FST is the better fit: exact, invertible, explicit abstention.

**Is OMW 'expand' sound for Greek?** Sound as a *construction method* (Bond & Foster, ACL 2013), unsound as a source of language-specific taxonomy without validation. Bond, Morgado da Costa, Goodman, McCrae & Lohk (LREC 2020) document the failure modes: cross-lingual mapping errors, uneven coverage, sense drift under polysemy. Arcan, McCrae & Buitelaar (COLING 2016) make the sharpest point: expansion requires translating *all* senses including rare ones, exactly where MT and bilingual dictionaries fail. For Greek the provenance matters: the OMW Greek wordnet is the BalkaNet resource (Stamou et al., GWC 2002) built at DBLab Patras, whose synset IDs are `ENG20` — Princeton **2.0** — listed at 18,049 synsets / 18,227 words / 24,106 senses with **57% core-synset coverage**. Lexorama expands it through omw-en:1.4 = PWN **3.0** structure. The critical methodological requirement the expand method makes easy to violate: relations hold between **synsets**, and a lemma participates only *via a sense*. Projecting synset relations onto bare lemma strings is not an approximation of WordNet — it is a different, much noisier relation. There is no published defence of doing this.

**Gaps vs Lexorama.** (1) There is no form→lemma *resolver* in the NLP sense: `main.py:301-315` resolves by `WHERE si.normalized_surface = ?` — a precomputed string index, no analysis, no context, no disambiguation model (3,663,525 index rows, 1,990,733 distinct keys, 254,101 keys (12.8%) mapping to >1 lemma). That is a full-form lexicon lookup — the Sgarbas 2000 DAWG design minus the DAWG — legitimate and even preferable for a dictionary, provided the project never claims lemmatization accuracy. It currently claims none, to its credit. (2) The 0.97 gate reproduces exactly (553,802 comparable cells, overall 0.9919; adj:-ος 0.9920, noun:-α 0.9979, -ή 0.9936, -η 0.9937, -ι 0.9945, -ί 0.9889, -μα 0.9963, -ο 0.9725, -ος 0.9813, -ά 0.9907, -ό 0.9978, verb:present 0.9790 — matching `HANDOFF.md:452-453` to 3 decimals) but is measured on the wrong population (F49), and the field standard holds out **lemmas**, not cells. Recall/coverage is never reported: 63,185 of 93,563 noun/adj/verb lemmas (67.5%) received cells, and even at 0.9725 that is ~450 wrong cells per 16k entering the searchable index. **UD_Greek-GUD is CC BY-SA 4.0 and available** — the project could measure against a gold standard today and does not. (3) The abstentions are honest but symptomatic of not being lexicon-conditioned: `paradigm_engine.py:96-108, 130-141` drop all -ος/-ο genitives; `:166-181` drops all -η plurals; `:144-163` drops -α gen.pl; `:302-303` refuses contract verbs. The project already *has* the per-stem evidence (2,587,575 el-wiktionary + 458,361 en-wiktionary forms), so the continuation class could be **induced** from stored forms per lemma rather than guessed from the ending — the single highest-value methodological upgrade available. (4) The omw-el import (F53). (5) The flagship accent-homograph invariant is untestable because the words are missing (F21): `normalize_greek.py:16-18, 60-64` names πότε/ποτέ and νόμος/νομός as the motivating pairs, and **ποτέ and νομός do not exist as lemmas**. (6) Index hygiene: 3,663,525 rows but only 2,380,103 distinct (lemma_id, surface, surface_type) — 35% redundancy — and English metalanguage from en-Wiktionary conjugation tables is stored as **forms** and is searchable (`dependent (for simple past)`, `formed using present`, `present perfect from above with a particle (να, ασ).`, each mapping to ~900 lemmas); 354,631 forms contain Latin letters and 364,351 contain spaces.

### 4.5 Computational lexicography and dictionary UX

**Verdict: marginal.** Defensible as a licensed Wiktionary+corpus aggregator with unusually honest provenance labelling; indefensible as "corpus-driven lexicography" in the Sketch Engine tradition. The collocation layer is a pre-Word-Sketch untyped co-occurrence bag, the example extractor is a target-blind GDEX subset missing GDEX's single most important feature (rare-word penalty), and the entire lexical model is lemma-level where the literature and OntoLex-Lemon are sense-level. There is zero manual evaluation of lexicographic content quality and zero user research, despite the project already logging exactly the data Lew's log-file paradigm needs.

**Word Sketches.** Since the mid-1990s the reference architecture has been the *Word Sketch*: a one-page, grammatically organised summary of a headword's collocational behaviour, produced by running a language-specific *sketch grammar* over a lemmatised, POS-tagged corpus and ranking collocates by a salience statistic. Kilgarriff, Rychlý, Smrž & Tugwell (EURALEX 2004) is the canonical system description; the method was first used in production for the Macmillan English Dictionary. The load-bearing claim is not "collocation is useful" but that **grammatically typed** collocation (OBJECT_OF, ADJ_MODIFIER, AND/OR, MODIFIES) is qualitatively more useful than an untyped window or sentence co-occurrence list, because it separates argument structure from modification and exposes sense distinctions. The literature also supplies a hard evaluation number: Kilgarriff, Kovář, Krek, Srdanović & Tiberius (EURALEX 2010, 372–379) ran a *user* evaluation — 42 headwords per language (18 nouns / 12 verbs / 12 adjectives, stratified across frequency bands), top 20 collocates, 2–3 expert annotators, criterion "would this collocation have been suitable for an entry in a collocations dictionary like the OCD?". Restricting to full agreement: Dutch **66.3%** good (332/501), English **70.7%** (367/519), Slovene **71.1%** (391/550), Japanese 94.6% on 3-way agreement. The defensible-practice bar is therefore ~two-thirds publishable precision, **measured**, with the protocol stated. Reported failure causes were POS/lemmatisation errors, corpus duplication and web junk.

**Examples.** GDEX (Kilgarriff, Husák, McAdam, Rundell & Rychlý, EURALEX 2008, 425–432) is the standard for automatic example selection: a weighted heuristic combining an optimal length interval (Sketch Engine's documented sample uses `optimal_interval(length, 10, 14)`, penalising below 5 and above 28 tokens), a whole-sentence check, blacklists of illegal characters, *greylists* with per-occurrence penalties (0.1 per rare character, 0.1 per pronoun tag — the anaphora proxy), and `keyword_position`, explicitly a function of *where the headword sits*. Two features do most of the work and are routinely under-appreciated: the **rare-word criterion** (a sentence whose other words are outside a frequency list is a bad example even if perfectly formed) and the **pronoun/anaphora penalty**. Kosem, Koppel, Zingano Kuhn, Michelfeit & Tiberius (*IJL* 32(2):119–137, 2019) is the sobering follow-up: GDEX configurations do not transfer across languages or dictionary purposes, inter-lexicographer agreement on "good example" is low, and the notion is target-audience-relative. Not contested: example scoring must be **per headword and per sense**, not per sentence.

**Senses.** Kilgarriff's "I don't believe in word senses" (*Computers and the Humanities* 31(2):91–113, 1997) is the foundational sceptical text: senses are abstractions over clusters of corpus citations, relative to a purpose. The architectural consequence, widely accepted even by those who reject the rhetoric: a modern dictionary must (a) state its granularity policy, (b) give senses stable identifiers so examples, collocates, translations and relations can be *attached* to them, and (c) treat the inventory as revisable data.

**Database vs text.** de Schryver (*IJL* 16(2):143–199, 2003) framed the shift; the settled position is that a born-digital dictionary is a *lexicographic database* from which presentation texts are generated. Tarp & Gouws (*Lexikos* 33(1):425–447, 2023) push further. The interoperability layer is OntoLex-Lemon (Cimiano, McCrae & Buitelaar, W3C Ontology-Lexica CG Final Report, 10 May 2016) with `LexicalEntry` / `Form` / `LexicalSense` / `LexicalConcept`, extended by *lexicog* (Bosque-Gil & Gracia, 17 Sept 2019) adding `LexicographicResource`, `Entry`, `LexicographicComponent`, `FormRestriction` and `UsageExample`. ELEXIS (Krek et al., EURALEX 2018) operationalised this into shared infrastructure and a cross-dictionary linking matrix. For "typed + sourced" claims the standard is PROV-O (W3C Recommendation, 30 April 2013): Entity/Activity/Agent, `wasGeneratedBy`, `wasDerivedFrom`, `wasAttributedTo`. **A source string is not provenance; provenance names the process and its parameters.**

**Users.** Hartmann (*Teaching and Researching Lexicography*, Routledge 2001) codified dictionary-use research; Tarp's function theory (2008) insists a dictionary is designed against declared *functions* (text reception, production, translation, cognitive) and user situations — you cannot evaluate a dictionary without naming what it is for. This is contested: Tarp models what users *need*; Lew's empirical programme measures what they *do*. Lew & de Schryver (*IJL* 27(4):341–359, 2014) survey the digital user; Lew (*IJL* 28(1), 2015) established the log-file paradigm on Wiktionary; Lew, Kaźmierczak, Tomczak & Leszkowicz (*IJL* 31(1):53–77, 2018) used eye-tracking to show definitions and pictorial illustrations **compete** for attention rather than reinforcing; Lew & Wolfer (*SAGE Open* 14(1), 2024) modelled which lexical factors drive look-ups. The recognised evaluative repertoire is: lookup success rate, time-to-answer, comprehension/production task outcomes, log-file coverage of unresolved queries, and expert precision sampling of automatically generated content — not internal statistics.

**Gaps vs Lexorama.** (1) Collocation is untyped — `ingest_collocations.py` reads Leipzig `*-co_s.txt` and stores a flat bag (`schema.sql:135-144`, no `relation_type` column); no sketch grammar, no dependency parse; coverage is only 37,662 of 446,840 lemmas (8.4%); no dispersion gate although Gries DP is already implemented for keyness (`main.py:1407`); **no manual precision evaluation anywhere in the repo** against the literature bar of ≥66%. (2) The example scorer is target-blind: `ingest_examples.py:64` `def sentence_quality(sentence: str, n_words: int)` takes **no headword argument**, and `:129-145` offers the same score to every lemma the sentence contains; missing GDEX criteria are the rare-word check (the project *has* a frequency table and does not use it here), the pronoun/anaphora penalty, and `keyword_position`; the blacklist is only http/www./@ and a >15% digit ratio (`:77-81`). Defensible: the length window (`:45-47`) is close to SkE's, and the whole-sentence check (`:72-75`) correctly handles the Greek question mark. (3) Everything attaches to the lemma: `corpus_examples(lemma_id, …)`, `collocations(lemma_id, …)`, `relations(source_lemma_id, …)`, `semantic_neighbors(lemma_id, …)`, `lemma_domain_pred(lemma_id, …)`. **32,189 lemmas have BOTH ≥2 senses and ≥1 corpus example** — for every one, `WordPage.tsx:256-283` renders examples unattached to the sense they illustrate. 55,360 synonym edges are lemma-to-lemma, which is semantically wrong: synonymy holds between senses. (4) No stable sense identifier is exposed: `main.py:548-557` emits `{index, gloss, tags, examples, source}` and never surfaces `senses.id` (`schema.sql:20`), foreclosing ELEXIS-style linking. (5) The sense inventory is inherited, not constructed, with no stated policy: 541,759 senses over 444,124 sense-bearing lemmas; **386,244 lemmas (87.0%) have exactly one sense**; 496,018 senses (91.6%) from el-wiktionary; el and en inventories are rendered as two separate lists (`WordPage.tsx:143-144`) with no merge. **`MethodologyPage.tsx` says nothing at all about senses, examples, collocations or definitions** — it is a methodology page for the statistics layer, not for the dictionary. (6) Provenance is a string, not a process model: nothing records that `collocations.score` came from `ingest_collocations.py` with `top_n=12` and that stopword list, or that `lemma_domain_pred` classifier rows come from a model with held-out 0.560/0.463. Compounding this, the UI badges a whole block from its **first** item — `WordPage.tsx:269` `<SourceBadge source={entry.examples[0].source} />`, identically at `:461` and `:501` — accidentally correct today because each block is single-source, and silently wrong the moment a second source is ingested. (7) No standards export: no OntoLex-Lemon/lexicog serialisation, no RDF, no persistent URIs, no TEI-Lex0, although the mapping is nearly trivial (`lemmas`→`ontolex:LexicalEntry`, `forms`→`ontolex:Form`, `senses`→`ontolex:LexicalSense`, `corpus_examples`→`lexicog:UsageExample`). For a project pitched as "free open-data" this is the largest reputational gap. (8) Cross-language search bypasses senses: `gloss_index(en_term, lemma_id, rank_weight, source)` — 58,458 rows, 34,988 distinct terms, up to **58 lemmas per single English term** — points at lemmas, so a user typing "kite" cannot be told which Greek sense translates it. (9) No evaluation of the dictionary as a dictionary and no user research: `query_log` (`schema.sql:296-307`) plus `/api/insights/queries` (`main.py:676-746`) is precisely Lew's substrate and is described in the schema comment as a "data flywheel", but no lookup-success rate, no unresolved-query coverage metric and no task-based evaluation is computed from it; and per Tarp the project never declares its lexicographic function, so it cannot be evaluated on its own terms.

**Where Lexorama is defensible.** Separating curated `senses.examples` from mined `corpus_examples` (`schema.sql:150-151`) is exactly the right instinct. The licensing wall plus `source_attributions` with `license` and `retrieved_at` per source is materially better than typical hobby aggregators. Marking inferred data as inferred (`lemma_domain_pred.source = 'classifier'`; the amber block at `MethodologyPage.tsx:215-229`) matches Rundell's post-editing framing minus the human gate. Refusing to calibrate a weak classifier's probabilities is a defensible call, if for the wrong reason (see §4.6). The corpus-tagging discipline on diachronic tables (`schema.sql:179-184`) — never pooling registers — is methodologically stronger than the lexicographic layer beside it.

**The structural finding.** The project has spent nearly its entire rigor budget on the diachronic/statistical layer (bootstrap CIs, Dubossarsky controls, BH-FDR, Theil–Sen, Mann–Kendall, Gries DP, Hardie log-ratio) and almost none on the lexicography, while presenting itself as a dictionary. The asymmetry is inverted relative to what users of a dictionary will actually consult.

### 4.6 ML evaluation rigor: distant supervision, calibration, small-sample metrics

**Verdict: marginal, with three indefensible specifics.** The posture — distant supervision, abstention, provenance separation, an amber warning telling users the accuracy is 0.56 — is more honest than typical hobby-project ML. But: (1) the protocol is a single seed-0 split also used to select hyperparameters, deprecated since Dietterich 1998 and Cawley & Talbot 2010, with no interval despite per-class test support in single digits; (2) no precision-at-threshold or risk–coverage estimate exists, so the accuracy shown to users describes 100% coverage while the system ships a ~31% coverage subset; (3) a raw softmax from a `class_weight='balanced'` fit is rendered as «εμπιστοσύνη N%», which Elkan 2001 and Dal Pozzolo et al. 2015 establish is not a probability, and the stated reason for not calibrating **inverts what calibration does**. Compounding all three, the live database was written by pre-#47 code, so the margin-gate claim at `MethodologyPage.tsx:221` is false for the data actually served.

**1. Distant supervision.** The paradigm originates with Mintz, Bills, Snow & Jurafsky (ACL-IJCNLP 2009, 1003–1011). The field's subsequent history is a history of correcting its assumption. Riedel, Yao & McCallum (ECML PKDD 2010) named the flaw: distant supervision assumes every instance matching the knowledge base expresses the labelled relation, which is false and "leads to noisy patterns that hurt precision". Roth, Barth, Wiegand & Klakow (AKBC@CIKM 2013, 73–78) classify the entire remediation literature into three families (at-least-one constraints, topic models, pattern correlations) — the existence of that survey is the field's verdict that raw distant supervision is not deployable without noise handling. Two pathologies must be separated: **label noise** (the heuristic label is wrong for this instance) and **selection bias** (the set of instances that *receive* a label is not a random sample of the deployment population). The latter is more insidious and less often addressed: a crowd-built lexicon covers what its editors found salient, so a classifier inherits that coverage prior and its held-out estimate is computed on the *biased* distribution. A held-out number from distantly supervised gold is an **upper bound** on deployment accuracy, never an estimate of it.

**2. Evaluation protocol.** A single random split is the weakest defensible protocol and is explicitly deprecated. Dietterich (*Neural Computation* 10(7):1895–1923, 1998) showed both the difference-of-two-proportions test and the paired t-test over repeated random splits have unacceptable Type-I error and "should never be used", recommending 5×2cv or McNemar. Demšar (*JMLR* 7:1–30, 2006) normalised the expectation that *any* reported comparison carries a test. Cawley & Talbot (*JMLR* 11:2079–2107, 2010) demonstrated the second half: when a hyperparameter is chosen by looking at a performance estimate, that estimate becomes optimistically biased, and the bias can be "comparable in magnitude to differences between learning algorithms" — remedy, nested CV with an outer loop untouched by selection. Forman & Scholz (*SIGKDD Explorations* 12(1):49–57, 2010) add that all but one common way of aggregating F-measure across folds is biased under class imbalance. Dror, Baumer, Shlomov & Reichart (ACL 2018, 1383–1392) codified test selection for NLP. The consensus for a small tabular classifier: repeated stratified k-fold, hyperparameters selected in an inner loop, per-class support reported, and a dispersion estimate on every headline number.

**3. Interval estimation at small n.** Accuracy is a binomial proportion, and Brown, Cai & DasGupta (*Statistical Science* 16(2):101–133, 2001) showed the Wald interval's coverage is so erratic it should be abandoned; use Wilson or Jeffreys at small n. Macro-F1 has no closed-form interval — bootstrap over the test set (≥1,000 resamples). Macro-F1 is also definitionally ambiguous: Opitz & Burst (arXiv:1911.03347, 2019) show the two common definitions can differ by as much as 0.5 and can *reverse rankings*; they recommend the arithmetic mean, which is scikit-learn's `average='macro'`. The instability with rare classes is arithmetic: macro-averaging weights an 8-example class equally with a 600-example class, and a per-class precision on <20 test items has a 95% Wilson interval spanning roughly ±0.2–0.35. Reporting such a number to three decimals without an interval misrepresents the evidence.

**4. Calibration.** Platt (1999) scaling and isotonic regression (Zadrozny & Elkan, KDD 2002, 694–699) are the canonical post-hoc maps; Niculescu-Mizil & Caruana (ICML 2005, 625–632) established that logistic regression trained on log-loss is among the *better*-calibrated families out of the box. Guo, Pleiss, Sun & Weinberger (ICML 2017) formalised ECE/reliability diagrams as the reporting standard and showed single-parameter temperature scaling usually suffices; Kull et al. (NeurIPS 2019) extended this natively to multiclass. Nixon et al. (CVPR Workshops 2019) showed fixed-width ECE binning is biased. What is **not** contested, and is load-bearing here: re-weighting or resampling classes changes the training prior and therefore warps the posterior. Elkan (IJCAI 2001) gave the theorem relating a rescaled prior to the decision threshold; Dal Pozzolo, Caelen, Johnson & Bontempi (IEEE SSCI 2015, 159–166) gave the explicit correction, noting undersampling "warps the posterior probabilities away from the true probabilities". A classifier fit with balanced class weights emits scores that are **ranking-valid and probability-invalid**.

**5. Thresholds and abstention.** Chow (*IEEE Trans. Inf. Theory* 16(1):41–46, 1970) derived the optimal error/reject tradeoff; El-Yaniv & Wiener (*JMLR* 11:1605–1641, 2010) formalised the risk–coverage curve; Geifman & El-Yaniv (NeurIPS 2017) showed how to *guarantee* a target risk with high probability at maximal coverage. The standard is unambiguous: a rejection threshold is a hyperparameter, it must be chosen on data disjoint from the data used to report the resulting risk, and the quantity to report is **precision (or risk) at the operating coverage**.

**Gaps vs Lexorama.** All measured read-only against the live DB. **G1** — the shipped data does not match the shipped code: `CONF_FLOOR = 0.50` at `classify_domains.py:69-71` and `:258` refuses to store below it, yet the table has `MIN(score) = 0.30` and **10,251/16,803 (61.0%)** below 0.50; the write path is DELETE-then-rewrite (`:218`), so every row was written by pre-#47 code whose floor was 0.30 (`HANDOFF.md:593`). **No row in the database was ever margin-filtered** — margin is not stored and cannot be recovered — while `MethodologyPage.tsx:221` tells users predictions are kept only «με σαφές περιθώριο από τη δεύτερη επιλογή». **G2** — HANDOFF is stale (17,699 / 5,601 claimed vs 16,803 / 5,152 actual), and no artifact ties metrics to data: `classify_domains()` returns its metrics dict to stdout (`:278-292`) and nothing persists it; there is no eval JSON under `data/processed/`; `tests/test_domains.py` asserts nothing about accuracy, thresholds or calibration. The headline "0.560 / 0.463" exists solely as prose in `HANDOFF.md:584` and `MethodologyPage.tsx:217` and is **not reproducible from the repository**. **G3** — single split, seed 0, used for both selection and reporting (`:191-193`; `HANDOFF.md:583-584` records skip-gram chosen because held-out accuracy went 0.478→0.560 on that split; `C=4.0` at `:195` is a tuned constant with no recorded search). With n_test ≈ 997 the 95% Wilson interval on 0.560 is ≈ ±0.031. **G4** — per-class precision reported at sample sizes carrying almost no information: gold support ranges medicine 3,242 down to **economics 49**, sports 167, military 231; at 80/20 that is ~10 test items for economics *before* the embedding filter, and the filter retains only **4,983 of 12,379 (40.3%)**, so economics' real test support is plausibly 3–5. A precision of "0.400" on 5 trials has a 95% Wilson interval of roughly [0.12, 0.77]. `zero_division=0` (`:204`) reports 0.000 for a never-predicted class, indistinguishable from genuine failure, and no support column is emitted. **G5** — that 60% attrition is a second, undisclosed selection bias, anti-correlated with exactly the technical vocabulary the fields are made of and not random with respect to class; neither MethodologyPage nor HANDOFF discloses it. **G6** — the predicted marginal is visibly flattened: gold prior spans **66:1**, predictions span **2.4:1**; philosophy ranks 15th of 18 in gold but **1st** among predictions; economics has 76 gold rows but **636** classifier predictions. **G7** — the uncalibrated score is nonetheless rendered as a percentage (`main.py:1277` returns `domain_score`; `ExplorePage.tsx:332` renders «εμπιστοσύνη 77%»). The stated justification (`classify_domains.py:26-27, 210-212, 285`; `MethodologyPage.tsx:219-220`) inverts the logic: calibration does not inflate confidence — Platt/isotonic/Dirichlet fitting typically *lowers* overconfident scores and leaves ranking unchanged. Refusing to calibrate while still printing a percentage is the worst of both. **G8** — no precision-at-threshold estimate anywhere; the fix requires no retraining beyond what already runs (apply the same threshold to `clf_eval.predict_proba(Xte)`, report precision/coverage at 0.30/0.50/0.55/0.70, bootstrap a CI).

**Where the project is defensible.** Two things are genuinely right and rarely are: the prediction vector is built exactly like the training vector (both L2-normalized, `:182-186` vs `:244-247`, so there is **no train/serve skew**), and the embedding key correctly uses `normalize_keep_accents` (`:163`) rather than the accent-folding key. `average='macro'` is the definition Opitz & Burst recommend. Choosing per-class **precision** as the headline is the right metric for an additive feature. Excluding multi-tag lemmas from training while retaining them as gold (`:170-171`, `:221-229`) is a sensible cheap noise reduction. The amber warning is real honesty — the problem is that the honest number is measured on the wrong population at the wrong coverage.

### 4.7 LLM grounding, attribution, and hallucination evaluation

**Verdict: mixed, and the mix matters.** On the substrate — per-item provenance — Lexorama is **defensible** and ahead of the RAG literature's normal baseline. On the claim it advertises — "AI explains; sources define" — the verdict is **indefensible as stated**, because there is no AI. On the one place a grounding-style claim *is* made about generated content — the ≥0.97 paradigm gate licensing 170,749 `source='generated'` cells — the verdict is **marginal-to-indefensible on method**.

**What the literature establishes.** The modern grounding stack begins with Lewis et al. (NeurIPS 2020, 9459–9474), which fused a DPR retriever with a BART generator and showed conditioning on retrieved passages made outputs "more factual and specific". That paper established retrieval as an architecture, not an evaluation. The evaluation half arrived with Rashkin et al.'s **AIS** framework (*Computational Linguistics* 49(4):777–840, 2023), the single most important reference for a project whose slogan is "sources define, AI explains". AIS is deliberately narrow and therefore usable: a sentence is *Attributable to Identified Sources* iff a generic hearer would agree that "According to [source], S" is true, after a first-stage **interpretability screen** that discards sentences too ambiguous or context-dependent to judge. Two design decisions deserve copying: AIS is binary and evidence-relative, not a truth judgement; and the two-stage pipeline separates "is this even a checkable proposition?" from "is it supported?" — a distinction that matters enormously for pedagogical prose, much of which is hedging, framing or didactic scaffolding rather than assertion.

Bohnet et al. (arXiv:2212.08037) operationalised AIS as Attributed QA and contributed AutoAIS, an NLI-style automatic proxy. Their numbers set the standard caveat: AutoAIS correlates with human AIS at Pearson **0.96 at the system level**, but instance-level correlation is "much lower and more variable". Their best system (retrieve-then-read) reached **AIS 65.5% ± 1.5** on Natural Questions, rising to 71.4% with AutoAIS reranking — roughly a third of attributed answers from a strong 2022 system were *not* supported by their own citations. Gao et al.'s **ALCE** (EMNLP 2023, 6465–6488) generalised this into fluency/correctness/citation-quality triples and found that on ELI5 **even the best models lack complete citation support 50% of the time**. Luyu Gao et al.'s RARR (ACL 2023, 16477–16508) is the post-hoc complement: research the claim, then minimally edit the output to restore attribution.

For meta-evaluation of attribution itself, Yue et al. (Findings of EMNLP 2023, 4615–4635) built AttrScore over a 4-way label set (attributable / extrapolatory / contradictory / non-attributable). On the ~242-example AttrEval-GenSearch set, GPT-4 reached only **~81–83% accuracy**, and the best fine-tuned model ~75.2% F1, with *contradictory* the hardest class. That is the ceiling on automated attribution judging, and it is not high enough to be the sole gate.

Hallucination taxonomy is settled enough to be useful: Ji et al. (*ACM Computing Surveys* 55(12), Art. 248, 2023) fixed the intrinsic (contradicts source) vs extrinsic (unverifiable from source) split; Huang et al. (*ACM TOIS* 43(2), Art. 42) extended it to factuality vs faithfulness. Faithfulness metrics converge on three families: NLI/alignment (AlignScore, ACL 2023, 11328–11348 — 355M params, 4.7M training examples from 7 tasks, evaluated on 22 datasets, matching GPT-4-based metrics), QA-based (QAFactEval, NAACL 2022, 2587–2601, +14% over prior QA metrics), and sampling-consistency (SelfCheckGPT, EMNLP 2023, 9004–9017). **FActScore** (Min et al., EMNLP 2023, 12076–12100) is the decomposition primitive that matters most here: split a generation into *atomic facts* and score precision against a knowledge source; the automated estimator has <2% error against human FActScore, and ChatGPT scored 58% on biographies. RAGTruth (ACL 2024, 10862–10878; ~18k responses, word-level span annotation) and RAGAS (EACL 2024 demos, 150–158) supply the corpus and the reference-free harness.

LLM-as-judge is usable but must be caged. Zheng et al. (NeurIPS 2023 D&B) report >80% GPT-4/human agreement — the same level as human/human — while documenting position, verbosity and self-enhancement biases. Panickssery, Bowman & Feng (NeurIPS 2024) showed self-preference is **causally linked to self-recognition**, so a judge from the same family as the generator is compromised. Bavaresco et al. (JUDGE-BENCH, ACL 2025 short) evaluated 11 LLMs on 20 annotated datasets and concluded LLMs are not ready to replace human judges systematically — and correlation with **expert** annotators is consistently lower than with non-experts, which is precisely the regime of Greek grammatical explanation. None of these benchmarks include Greek.

Abstention is the third leg. Kalai, Nachum, Vempala & Zhang (arXiv:2509.04664) argue hallucination is a rational response to binary-scored benchmarks that reward guessing over "I don't know", implying an eval harness must **credit** abstention. Feng et al. (ACL 2024, 14664–14690, SAC award) get up to +19.3% abstain accuracy via multi-LLM probing. Quach et al. (ICLR 2024) give distribution-free conformal guarantees over sampled candidate sets. Finally, the strongest available result: when a *deterministic* checker exists, do not evaluate — **constrain**. Geng, Josifoski, Peyrard & West (EMNLP 2023, 10932–10952) show grammar-constrained decoding makes malformed structured output impossible rather than merely rare.

**Gaps vs Lexorama.** The headline finding is that **Lexorama has no LLM anywhere**, so its central slogan is currently unfalsifiable marketing (see §3.9 for the grep evidence). What the code calls "explanation" is a deterministic f-string at `main.py:266-274`. **Where the project is genuinely ahead:** the precondition most RAG systems lack — a corpus where every atom is already source-attributed — Lexorama has, and it is verified: `source TEXT NOT NULL` on senses (`schema.sql:26`), forms (36), relations (48), collocations (156); **0 null/empty sources across 541,759 senses, 3,216,685 forms, 291,208 relations and 294,045 collocations**; clean source distributions; generated cells visually marked (`InflectionTables.tsx:74-80`). In AIS terms the retrieval corpus is fully "identified-source" by construction — a materially stronger starting position than ALCE's or RAGTruth's. **Gap 1** — the deterministic verifier is not exposed as a verifier: `paradigm_engine.generate()` (`:320`) is write-time only, imported by `backfill_paradigms.py` and `validate_paradigms.py` and nowhere else; there is no `verify(lemma, form, features) -> bool` and no endpoint a generated explanation could be checked against. Geng et al. say: where a grammar exists, constrain rather than evaluate. Lexorama has the grammar and does not expose it — the single highest-leverage change. **Gap 2** — the ≥0.97 bar is not enforced anywhere in code (`backfill_paradigms.py:4` is a docstring; `validate_paradigms.py:120-125` only prints; no threshold comparison, no assertion, no stored artifact, no exit code; `backfill()` has no per-class allow-list). **Gap 3** — the precision estimate is measured on a population disjoint from the one it licenses (F49); this is a textbook covariate-shift error and the same structural bug AIS-style evaluation is designed to catch: the thing you measured is not the thing you published. For calibration, Stanza on UD Greek GDT reports UFeats 94.72 and Lemmas 96.05 — Lexorama claims to beat a well-tuned neural morphological tagger on the *harder, unseen* half of the distribution on the strength of an estimate taken from the easier half. **Gap 4** — no eval harness, no gold set, no abstention semantics; the one confidence gate that exists (0.55 display / 0.30 store) is hand-picked with no calibration analysis, where Quach et al. show how to set such a threshold with a distribution-free guarantee.

**The acceptance bar for any future explanation feature.** (1) Expose `paradigm_engine.verify()` and route every morphological claim through it; block on contradiction — target 100% of morphological atoms verifier-checked, not sampled. (2) Every non-morphological claim must resolve to a `(table, row_id, source)` triple or be abstained; report a per-claim AIS-style support rate with a floor no lower than the ALCE/Bohnet state of the art (≥70% citation recall is the *floor*, not the goal, given a fully attributed corpus). (3) A human-annotated Greek gold set of ≥200–300 explanations, span-annotated in the RAGTruth style, two annotators, reported agreement. (4) Any LLM judge must be from a different family than the generator (Panickssery), tested for position/verbosity bias (Zheng), and treated as a screen validated against the gold set — its own ceiling is ~81–83% (Yue). (5) Fix Gap 3 first by re-running validation with a held-out slice of Wiktionary-covered cells *masked* before generation. (6) Turn `validate_paradigms.py` into a pytest that fails below 0.97 per class and writes a JSON artifact, and add a per-class allow-list to `backfill_paradigms.py` read from that artifact.

### 4.8 Open-data licensing, share-alike propagation, and database rights

**Verdict: marginal overall, indefensible as currently documented on two axes.** The Wiktionary→CC BY-SA 4.0 axis is done to a genuinely good standard — better than most hobby projects and roughly at DBnary's level. But (a) two of the six external corpora appear to be NonCommercial-licensed (Leipzig Wortschatz: CC BY-NC; Greek Parliament Proceedings, Zenodo 4311577: CC BY-NC 4.0) while the code asserts "CC BY 4.0" or records nothing at all — and NC is flatly incompatible with the CC BY-SA 4.0 the project declares over its own derived DB; and (b) the repository ships **no LICENSE file**, so the CC BY-SA claim exists only as Greek prose in a React component.

**What the licence texts establish.** CC BY-SA 4.0 §3(a)(1) requires a reuser who *Shares* to retain creator identification, a copyright notice, a licence notice, a disclaimer notice, a URI/hyperlink, and — separately — to "indicate if You modified the Licensed Material". §3(b) requires any Adapted Material be released under "a Creative Commons license with the same License Elements … or a BY-SA Compatible License", with the text or URI of that Adapter's License included. The compatible-licence list has exactly **two** entries: Free Art License 1.3 (21 Oct 2014) and GPLv3 (8 Oct 2015, explicitly **one-way**). For BY-SA 3.0 the list is empty. Critically, "Share" is defined as providing material to the public "by any means or process that requires permission … such as reproduction, public display, public performance, distribution, dissemination, communication, or importation" — the WIPO making-available formulation. **CC BY-SA has no network-use loophole.** Unlike GPLv2/GPLv3 (where the AGPL exists precisely because network use is not distribution), serving Wiktionary-derived glosses over an HTTP API *is* Sharing.

**The database-rights layer.** CC 4.0 was the first international suite to licence sui generis database rights. §4 grants extraction/reuse/reproduction/Sharing of "all or a substantial portion of the database contents", then supplies the decisive rule: if you include a substantial portion in a database in which *you* hold SGDR, **that database (but not its individual contents) is Adapted Material for purposes of §3(b)**. So a merged lexical DB is an *adapted database*, not a mere "collection" — the collection escape hatch survives only where works sit side by side unmodified and unmerged, which a normalised, key-joined, cross-source lemma graph is not. "Substantial portion" has no bright line: CC states "there is no clearly defined rule or standard … determined both quantitatively and qualitatively. Also, using several insubstantial portions can add up to a substantial portion". The CJEU in *BHB v William Hill* (C-203/02, 2004) narrowed the *upstream* right via the obtaining/creating distinction — relevant because it weakens Lexorama's own SGDR claim over its derived tables (drift scores, cosine trajectories and classifier outputs are *created*, not obtained) while leaving the upstream corpora's rights intact. *CV-Online Latvia v Melons* (C-762/19, 2021) added a balancing test: extraction/re-utilisation is prohibitable only where it "adversely affects the investment" in obtaining, verifying or presenting — directly on point for an API re-serving 276,769 Leipzig sentences. Articles 3/4 of the DSM Directive (EU) 2019/790 expressly disapply Art. 7(1) of 96/9/EC for TDM, so **mining** (training word2vec, computing cosine distances, computing log-likelihood) is covered — but TDM exceptions do not launder the contract/licence terms attached to the source and do not cover redistributing the underlying sentences.

**Wiktionary's dual licence.** Wikimedia ToU §7 licenses contributed text under CC BY-SA 4.0 *and* GFDL (unversioned, no invariant sections), and reusers "may comply with either license or both". Attribution may be discharged by hyperlink to the article, hyperlink to a stable conforming copy, or a list of all authors. Note the asymmetry: text *imported into* Wikimedia under CC BY-SA-only terms is not available under GFDL. The CC BY-SA path is far cheaper — GFDL additionally demands the full licence text, a transparent copy, a History section, and five principal authors on the title page. DBnary (Sérasset, *SWJ* 6(4):355–361, 2015) is the canonical precedent and takes the CC BY-SA path explicitly.

**Multi-licence stacking.** Hagedorn et al. (*ZooKeys* 150:127–149, 2011) is the definitive treatment of why NC is corrosive in aggregated open data: NC is ill-defined, not equivalent to "non-profit", and cannot be absorbed into BY or BY-SA pools. Combining CC BY-SA (Wiktionary), CC BY and Apache-2.0 (OMW) into one served DB is **legal**, provided Apache §4 is honoured (copy of the licence, retained NOTICE, statement of changes) and the Princeton WordNet 3.0 BSD-style notice+disclaimer is reproduced. Adding **any NC-licensed source destroys this**: NC 4.0 §2(a)(1) limits Share/adapt to NonCommercial purposes only, which is an "additional or different term … that restrict[s] exercise of the rights granted under the Adapter's License", precisely what BY-SA §3(b) forbids.

**Gaps vs Lexorama.** See F24, F25 and F61 for the measured detail. Summary: (A) two NC-licensed sources sit inside a database declared CC BY-SA 4.0 at `LicensingPage.tsx:55-56` and `docs/licensing.md:52-54` — Parliament (1,365,650 rows: 1,064,647 `frequency_timeseries` + 292,573 `diachronic_trajectory` + 8,430 `diachronic_drift`) and Leipzig (asserted as flat "CC BY 4.0" at `ingest_kaikki.py:92, 102, 112`, load-bearing for 276,769 verbatim sentences, 294,045 collocations, 185,027 neighbours and the whole news axis). The TDM angle partially rescues the derived *statistics* but does not rescue `main.py:505`, which serves verbatim Leipzig sentences to the public. (B) `record_attribution` no-ops for 7 of 14 source tags. (C) The gate test is too narrow to catch (B): `tests/test_pipeline_e2e.py:138-154` asserts only that `el-wiktionary` has a row; the invariant "every distinct `source` value in any content table has a `source_attributions` row with a non-empty licence" is not tested, and that one query would have caught the Parliament omission. (D) No LICENSE file, no `LICENSES/` directory, no copy of CC BY-SA 4.0, Apache-2.0 or the WordNet 3.0 notice — and the *code* is also unlicensed, so "free open-data" is currently false of the software. (E) The footer under-attributes (`Footer.tsx:11-20` names only Βικιλεξικό), which is defensible under §3(a)(2) only while `/licensing` is complete, which per (B) it is not — and `/licensing` renders nothing if the API call fails. (F) Genuinely defensible: dual licence correctly identified (`ingest_kaikki.py:50,60`), explicit modification notices in Greek and English (`:54-55`, `:64-65`) satisfying §3(a)(1)(B), `retrieved_at` dating each extraction, idempotent upsert (`:133-146`), a persistent footer so deep-linked word pages carry the notice, the correct outbound path (CC BY-SA over GFDL), `docs/licensing.md:16-27` correctly walling off Triantafyllides/Academy/e-lexicon, and — importantly — treating the merged DB as *adapted material* rather than a *collection*, which is the legally correct reading of §4(b) and which many comparable projects get wrong in the other direction. (G) The project does not claim serving an API avoids distribution, which is right. The corollary it has not acted on is its own promise at `docs/licensing.md:14` — no snapshot exists, and per (A) that snapshot must be built only after the NC-derived layers are excluded.
